Antivirus software explained: what it protects against, and what's genuinely worth paying for

Modern operating systems already ship with reasonably capable built-in protection. The real comparison isn't "antivirus or nothing" — it's whether a paid suite's extra features actually solve a problem you have.

Covers: detection methods, performance impact, extras, pricing traps Best for: deciding whether you need paid antivirus at all

Antivirus software has an unusual job compared to most software categories: its entire purpose is to run constantly in the background, doing work you hope you never notice, while staying out of the way of everything else you're trying to do. That makes it one of the harder product categories to compare fairly, since the best possible outcome — nothing bad ever happening — looks identical to doing nothing at all. A product can be working perfectly and quietly for years, and the only sign would be the absence of a problem you'd never have known to expect otherwise.

This guide focuses on what actually differs between free built-in protection and paid third-party suites, how detection technology works well enough to trust the marketing claims, and which extra features are genuinely useful rather than padding out a subscription tier.

What antivirus software is actually defending against

Malware & viruses

Malicious software designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorised access to a system — the original, broadest category antivirus software was built for.

Ransomware

Software that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key. Modern suites include dedicated behavioural safeguards specifically for this threat.

Phishing sites

Fake websites designed to steal login credentials or payment details. Browser and email-level protection matters as much as the antivirus engine here.

Spyware & trackers

Software that monitors activity or collects data without consent, often bundled with otherwise legitimate-looking downloads.

Most everyday risk for a careful user actually comes from social engineering — phishing emails, fake download buttons, convincing scam messages — rather than a virus silently infecting a fully updated, patched system. This matters for the comparison, because it means software alone was never going to be a complete solution regardless of which product you choose; habits and software work together, not as substitutes for one another. A healthy dose of scepticism toward unexpected attachments, urgent-sounding messages, and offers that seem too good to be true remains one of the more effective, entirely free protections available, regardless of which antivirus product sits on top of it.

Built-in protection versus third-party suites

Windows comes with Microsoft Defender built in, and macOS includes Gatekeeper and XProtect. Both have improved substantially over the years and now perform respectably in independent testing — a meaningful change from a decade ago, when built-in protection was widely considered a bare minimum at best. For a single, regularly updated device used carefully, built-in protection genuinely covers a large share of everyday risk without any additional purchase. This shift is worth appreciating specifically because a lot of common advice about needing third-party antivirus predates these improvements and hasn't fully caught up with how capable built-in protection has become.

Where third-party suites still earn their price is in extra features beyond core malware detection: a bundled VPN, a password manager, identity or dark-web monitoring, parental controls, and centralised management across several devices under one account. None of these are things built-in protection was ever designed to provide, so the real comparison isn't "which detects more viruses" — detection rates between reputable options are usually close — but "which bundle of extra features, if any, do I actually need."

How detection actually works

Signature-basedMatches files against a database of known malware fingerprintsCan't catch brand-new, previously unseen threats

Behavioural monitoringWatches what a program actually does once running — encrypting many files rapidly, for instanceReacts to behaviour already in progress, not before it starts
Cloud-based lookupChecks a file's reputation against a live, constantly updated databaseRequires an internet connection to be fully effective

Modern antivirus products combine all four approaches rather than relying on any single one, which is part of why independent lab results, discussed next, are a more meaningful signal than any single technique's description on a marketing page.

Reading independent test results correctly

Organisations like AV-Test and AV-Comparatives run regular, standardised evaluations of major antivirus products, testing detection rates, false positives, and system performance impact under controlled conditions. These results are useful evidence and far more reliable than marketing claims, but it's worth reading them with a couple of caveats: they describe a specific product version tested during a specific period, and detection rates among reputable, well-established products are usually close enough that the difference rarely matters more than the extra features and usability differences between them. It's also worth checking results from more than one testing lab where possible, since methodologies differ slightly and a product's relative ranking can shift a little depending on which lab's specific test set and criteria were used.

A product scoring 99.7% versus 99.9% in a lab test is not a meaningful real-world difference for most users. Weigh usability, performance impact, and included extras more heavily than small gaps between top-tier products' detection scores.

System performance impact

A security suite that constantly slows down everyday tasks — file copying, application launches, browsing — creates a real cost even if it never actually stops a threat, since a program people find frustrating is a program people are more likely to disable or work around entirely. Independent labs measure this specifically, usually as an impact score alongside detection rates, and it's worth weighing seriously rather than assuming all products have a similarly light footprint. Full system scans in particular can vary a lot in how much they slow down a machine while running, and how well they schedule themselves around active use rather than interrupting it, which matters a great deal on older or lower-powered hardware where the difference is far more noticeable than on a fast, modern machine.

Mobile security: a different risk profile entirely

Phones face a meaningfully different threat landscape than desktop computers. iOS's app sandboxing and App Store review process make traditional virus infections rare in practice, and Apple doesn't allow third-party antivirus apps the same low-level system access they'd have on a desktop, which limits what a mobile security app can even do on iPhone. Android's more open app installation model creates more realistic room for malicious apps, particularly ones installed from outside the official Play Store, making a security app somewhat more relevant there — though sticking to the official app store and reviewing app permissions carefully covers most of the same risk without a dedicated product at all.

Extra features worth paying for, and ones that usually aren't

Worth it if...

You don't already have a password manager

A bundled password manager can be a genuine reason to choose a paid suite if you're not already using one.

Worth it if...

You manage several family devices

Centralised device management and parental controls add real convenience for households with multiple devices to oversee.

Often skippable

A bundled VPN

Usually a limited, slower version of a dedicated VPN service — fine for occasional use, but a poor substitute if you need a VPN regularly.

Often skippable

"System optimiser" and cleanup tools

Frequently oversell their impact; modern operating systems already manage storage and startup programs reasonably well on their own.

Subscription pricing: what to check before renewing

Security software pricing is notorious for steep renewal jumps after an initial discounted term, and for device-count limits that quietly force an upgrade to a higher tier as a household adds devices. Before subscribing, or before auto-renewal kicks in on an existing plan, it's worth checking three things specifically: what the actual renewal price will be (not the first-year promotional price), how many devices the plan genuinely covers, and whether features you assumed were included actually require a higher tier. Calendar reminders set slightly before a renewal date are a simple, effective way to catch an unwanted price jump before it charges automatically.

When free protection is genuinely enough

For a single, regularly updated device, careful browsing habits, and no specific need for the bundled extras discussed above, built-in operating system protection plus a password manager and healthy scepticism toward unexpected links and attachments covers the overwhelming majority of everyday risk. Paid suites make more sense for households managing several devices centrally, anyone who values a bundled VPN or identity monitoring specifically, or users who've had a genuine security incident and want the added peace of mind a dedicated product and its support resources provide. There's no shame in preferring that peace of mind either — for some users, the extra confidence a well-reviewed paid product provides is worth the cost on its own, separate from any measurable difference in actual protection.

Password reuse and account security beyond antivirus

A meaningful share of account compromises have nothing to do with malware at all — they come from reused passwords exposed in a breach at one service, then tried against other accounts the same person uses. Antivirus software, however good, doesn't address this risk category, since the "attack" happens entirely outside the protected device. A password manager, whether it's the one bundled with a security suite or a standalone product, solves this more directly by making a unique password for every account practical rather than something only security-conscious people bother maintaining. Two-factor authentication adds a further layer that antivirus software simply doesn't touch, requiring a second proof of identity — a code, a physical key, a biometric check — even if a password does leak. Together, unique passwords and two-factor authentication close a gap that no antivirus product, however capable, was ever designed to cover.

Firewalls: usually already handled

Older antivirus suites often marketed a bundled firewall as a headline feature, back when operating systems' own firewalls were less capable. Modern Windows and macOS both include a reasonably solid built-in firewall by default, which is one reason firewall functionality has become a much smaller differentiator between free and paid security products than it used to be. It's still worth confirming a firewall is actually enabled, since it's occasionally switched off by other software during installation, but a dedicated third-party firewall product is rarely necessary for typical home use anymore.

Browser-level protection matters as much as the antivirus engine

A large share of real-world scams and phishing attempts are caught, or missed, at the browser level rather than by the antivirus engine itself. Modern browsers include their own phishing and malicious-site warnings, updated continuously against known bad domains, and keeping a browser current matters as much for security as keeping the antivirus product itself updated. Browser extensions from security suites can add an extra layer of warning on top of this, though it's worth being selective about how many security-related extensions run simultaneously, since overlapping tools occasionally conflict or slow down page loading without adding meaningful additional protection. Email providers have made similar improvements, filtering an increasing share of phishing attempts before they ever reach an inbox, which further shifts where the effective protection in a typical setup actually comes from.

Backups: the safety net antivirus can't fully replace

No security product, however well-reviewed, offers a 100% guarantee against every possible threat, which is why regular backups remain a necessary complement rather than an optional extra. For ransomware specifically, a recent backup stored separately from the main device — ideally offline or in a cloud service with version history — is often the difference between a serious incident and a minor inconvenience, since it removes the leverage a ransom demand depends on entirely. This is worth factoring into a security budget alongside antivirus software itself, since a backup routine can meaningfully reduce the real-world consequences of the small percentage of threats that get past any protection layer.

Why antivirus software looks different today than it used to

Antivirus products a decade or two ago worked primarily off signature databases, meaning protection was largely reactive — a threat had to be identified and catalogued somewhere before software could reliably catch it elsewhere. This left a real gap for brand-new, previously unseen threats, sometimes called zero-day attacks, which could spread for a period before defences caught up. The shift toward heuristic and behavioural detection, described earlier, exists specifically to close that gap, watching for suspicious patterns and actions rather than only matching against a known list. This evolution is also why older reputation for antivirus being unavoidably heavy and disruptive has softened considerably — modern detection methods are generally lighter on system resources than the older approach of scanning every file against an ever-growing signature database.

Choosing between similarly rated paid suites

Once a product clears the baseline of solid, independently verified detection and reasonable performance impact, the decision between competing paid suites usually comes down to the specific extras bundled in, the clarity and fairness of the pricing structure, and how well the interface fits your comfort level with technology. A suite aimed at less technical users often simplifies settings into a few clear toggles, while one aimed at more advanced users exposes granular controls that a casual user would never need to touch. Neither approach is wrong, but mismatching the product to your own comfort level can lead to either frustration (an overly simplified product that hides a setting you wanted) or unnecessary complexity (an advanced product with more configuration than you'll ever use).

Customer support quality is also worth a brief look before subscribing, particularly for less technical users — a company with responsive support and clear documentation genuinely matters if something ever goes wrong, such as a false positive blocking a legitimate program you rely on for work.

False positives: an underrated frustration

A security product flagging a legitimate, harmless file or program as a threat — a false positive — is more than a minor annoyance in practice. It can block a program you rely on, quarantine a file you need immediately, or, in more disruptive cases, interfere with specialised software used for work or creative projects. Independent lab tests measure false-positive rates specifically alongside detection rates, and a product with an excellent detection score but a poor false-positive record can be more disruptive to actual daily use than a slightly less aggressive product that leaves legitimate software alone. This is worth checking directly rather than assuming a high detection percentage tells the whole story on its own.

Common misconceptions worth retiring

A higher lab-tested detection percentage between two reputable, well-established products rarely translates into a meaningful real-world difference — usability, performance impact, and included extras usually matter more day to day than a fractional gap in a controlled test. Antivirus software was never designed to stop every threat on its own; phishing and social-engineering attacks specifically target human judgment rather than a file a scanner could catch, which is why habits remain part of the equation regardless of which product you use. And a free antivirus product isn't automatically inferior to a paid one for core protection — many free tiers from reputable companies perform comparably in independent testing, with the paid tiers mainly adding the extra features discussed above rather than fundamentally better detection.

Bottom line: built-in operating system protection covers most everyday risk on its own for a careful, regularly updated user. Paid suites earn their price mainly through extras like a VPN, identity monitoring, or centralised family device management — pay for the specific features you'll actually use, not for a marginally higher detection percentage.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need antivirus software if I already have Windows Defender?

For most careful, regularly updated users, Windows Defender provides solid baseline protection on its own. A paid suite makes more sense if you specifically want its bundled extras, like a VPN or centralised family device management.

Does antivirus software slow down my computer?

Some impact is unavoidable since the software runs constantly in the background, but the degree varies significantly between products. Independent lab tests measure this specifically and are worth checking before choosing a product.

Do I need antivirus software on my phone?

Generally less necessary than on a desktop, especially on iPhone, where Apple's app review process and sandboxing limit what a security app could even do. On Android, sticking to the official Play Store and reviewing app permissions covers most everyday risk.

Is a bundled password manager as good as a standalone one?

Usually functional for basics, but standalone password managers often offer more advanced features like secure sharing, breach monitoring, and broader browser and app integration. If password management is a priority, comparing dedicated options separately is worth the extra step.

Do I still need backups if I have good antivirus software?

Yes. No security product guarantees complete protection, and for ransomware specifically, a recent separate backup is often what actually limits the damage rather than the antivirus software itself.

Practical checklist before subscribing

  • Check what your operating system's built-in protection already covers before assuming you need a paid product.
  • Identify which extra features (VPN, password manager, parental controls) you'd actually use regularly.
  • Check the real renewal price, not just the first-year promotional rate.
  • Confirm device-count limits match your actual household, not just your first device.
  • Weigh independent lab performance-impact scores alongside detection rates, not just detection alone.
  • Set a reminder before auto-renewal to catch unwanted price increases.
  • Check a product's false-positive rate alongside its detection rate, not detection alone.
  • Make sure a recent backup exists somewhere separate from the main device, regardless of which security product you choose.
