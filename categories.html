How a category actually gets built

Manufacturer specifications first

Every comparison starts from what the maker actually publishes — capacity, dimensions, claimed performance — since that's the one number both products share.

Independent reviews and owner reports

We cross-check specs against professional reviews and long-term owner feedback, looking specifically for where the two disagree.

Everyday trade-offs

Noise, maintenance, learning curve, resale value — the details that only show up after a few months of actually owning something.

A relative score, not an absolute one

The rating on a comparison reflects how clearly one product suits most buyers better than the other, not a fixed quality performance score.

Weighted toward daily use

A feature used once a year counts for less than one you'd touch every day, even if it looks more impressive on a spec sheet.

No hidden tie-breakers

When two products are genuinely close, the rating stays close too — we don't force a wider gap to make the conclusion feel more decisive.

Revisited on real triggers

A comparison gets a fresh look when a product is discontinued, replaced, or has a meaningful price change — not on a fixed calendar.

Reader reports matter

Messages through the contact page pointing out something outdated get read, and they do lead to pages being corrected.

Periodic full passes

Beyond individual triggers, older categories get a slower, periodic sweep to catch anything that's gone stale without anyone flagging it.

Choosing between two categories

Some purchases don't fit neatly into one category, and that's normal rather than a sign the directory is missing something. A few situations come up often enough that they're worth addressing directly.

A tablet that might replace a laptop

We file this under Smartphones & Tablets by default, since the hardware and update cycle behave more like a phone's, but if you're specifically weighing a tablet against a genuine laptop, the laptop comparisons will give you the more useful trade-offs on things like keyboard quality and multitasking.

A plug-in hybrid

These sit in Cars & EVs alongside full electric vehicles, since the buying decision — charging habits, running costs, resale value — has more in common with an EV purchase than with a purely combustion one, even though the vehicle itself is a mix of both. Where a plug-in hybrid is being cross-shopped against a traditional petrol or diesel model instead, the comparison will say so explicitly rather than assuming every reader is already sold on electrification, since that's a genuinely different decision with different priorities.

A gaming headset

We treat these as Audio rather than Gaming, because the comfort, sound signature and microphone quality questions are closer to a general headphone comparison than to a console or controller one.

A smart-home appliance with an app

These stay in Home Appliances even when the app and connectivity features are a big part of the pitch, since day-to-day satisfaction still comes down to how well the physical appliance does its actual job.

A phone camera versus a dedicated camera

This one usually isn't a fair comparison in either direction — a flagship phone camera and a mirrorless body solve different problems — so rather than forcing it into one category, we address it directly inside the relevant smartphone comparison whenever it's a genuinely common question for that specific model.

A subscription bundled with hardware

When a purchase is really a hardware decision with a subscription attached — a game console and its online service, for example — we keep the comparison in the hardware category and mention the subscription as one factor among several, rather than splitting it into a separate software write-up.

Notes on each category

Smartphones & Tablets

This is the category that changes fastest, since two or three flagship launches a year reshuffle which comparisons are actually relevant. We try to retire or clearly flag comparisons involving a phone that's been discontinued for more than a generation, rather than leaving an outdated match-up sitting at the top of search results.

Laptops & PCs

Less seasonal than phones — a laptop bought this year is a reasonable purchase for three to five years, so these comparisons stay relevant longer. The trade-off is that when a manufacturer does refresh a line, the change can be substantial enough that an older comparison stops being useful almost overnight.

Cars & EVs

The category where a comparison is least likely to age well on pricing alone, since incentives, tax treatment and financing offers shift by region and by month in a way that a spec sheet never captures. Treat the driving-experience conclusions as durable and the price figures as a starting point to verify locally.

Home Appliances

Product cycles here are slow, and a well-reviewed appliance can stay the sensible choice for several years without a newer model changing the calculation much. The main thing worth re-checking over time is consumable pricing, which can drift upward well after the appliance itself has stopped changing, sometimes enough to change which of two similarly priced machines actually costs less to own after a couple of years.

Audio

A category where personal fit genuinely matters as much as the review — ear shape and listening habits affect comfort and even perceived sound quality in ways a comparison article can describe but never fully replace for you. Treat these as a strong shortlist, not a final verdict.

Gaming

The category most affected by things outside the hardware itself — exclusive release schedules, subscription pricing changes, and platform-specific sales all shift the practical value of a console well after the comparison was written, so it's worth a quick check of current offers, current exclusive lineups and current subscription pricing before buying, even if the hardware comparison itself hasn't changed at all.

Software & Cloud Tools

Pricing tiers and feature sets here can change with a single company announcement, more often than in any other category we cover. We link out to the provider's current pricing page wherever possible, since that number is the one most likely to have moved since a comparison was published.

A few mistakes worth avoiding

Comparing launch-day prices only

Most products drop in price within the first six to twelve months. If a comparison from last year still quotes the original launch price, treat that figure as historical context, not something to budget around.

Reading only the highest-rated comparison in a category

A high rating usually means the two products compared are well matched, not that either one is objectively the best available. The lower-rated comparisons often contain the more decisive, clear-cut recommendations.

Skipping the category page entirely

Jumping straight to a single comparison can mean missing a more relevant match-up one click away — someone comparing two mid-range phones might not realise a newer budget option now covers the same ground for less.

Assuming a category page is exhaustive

We add comparisons as we write them, not as a complete catalogue of every product on the market. If something you're specifically looking for isn't listed yet, the contact page is the fastest way to ask for it.

Why these seven categories

The list isn't meant to mirror a retailer's navigation menu. We settled on these seven because they're the groupings where readers most often arrive already choosing between two specific, named products rather than browsing generally — which is exactly the situation a head-to-head comparison is built for. A category like "accessories" or "wearables" would technically be accurate for some of our older content, but in practice those searches tend to be broader and better served by a buying guide than a direct comparison, so we've kept them out of this directory for now rather than adding a category with only one or two thin entries in it. As the comparison library grows, splitting one of the busier sections — smartphones is the likeliest candidate — into a narrower pair of categories is something we'd rather do properly, once there's enough depth on both sides to make the split genuinely useful, than rush just to make the directory look more comprehensive than it currently is.

Questions about how categories work

Will you add more categories?

Probably, but only once there's enough genuine comparison content to justify a dedicated section — we'd rather fold a handful of related articles into an existing category than launch a near-empty one just to have more entries in this directory — a category page with two or three comparisons in it reads as unfinished, and readers can usually tell the difference.

Can a product move to a different category later?

Yes, occasionally. If a product genuinely straddles two categories and we picked the less useful one initially, we'll move it and update the internal links rather than leaving it somewhere that doesn't serve readers well.

Why do some categories have more comparisons than others?

Mostly reader demand and how fast a market moves — phones get compared more often simply because more people are actively deciding between two phones at any given moment than, say, two air purifiers.

What if I disagree with how a category is scored?

Tell us specifically why through the contact page — a category's average rating is just a summary of the comparisons inside it, and if one of those individual ratings looks wrong to you, that's exactly the kind of feedback that gets a page a second look.

Are these categories the same in every country?

The categories themselves are universal, but pricing, availability and even which models get sold varies by region — a car or appliance comparison written with one market in mind may reference trims or price points that don't exist where you're shopping, so treat the qualitative conclusions as more portable than the specific numbers.

Do subcategories exist within these seven?

Informally, yes — smartphones naturally splits into flagship and budget tiers, laptops into ultrabooks and workstation-class machines, and cars into everyday commuters and higher-performance models — but we haven't built separate pages for those splits yet, since the category page and the search box together already get most readers to the right comparison quickly enough without an extra layer of navigation to click through.

How this directory is kept honest

Structure over sales

Categories aren't ranked by ad revenue

Some categories on this site earn more through affiliate links than others simply because of how retailers structure their programs — electronics generally pay better than home appliances, for instance. That difference has no bearing on how many comparisons we write for a category or how prominently it appears in this directory. The order above reflects reader traffic and how much genuinely useful ground there is to cover, not which section is most profitable for us.

Numbers on this page are directional

The comparison counts and average ratings shown throughout this page are accurate as of our last full review, but they shift slightly as new comparisons get published and older ones get retired. Treat them as a reasonable snapshot of how active a category is rather than a live, second-by-second count — if you need the exact current figure for a specific comparison, the individual article itself is always the more accurate source.

No category is quietly sponsored

None of the seven sections here exist because a brand paid for placement, and none of them will in the future without it being disclosed clearly on the page itself. If that ever changes for a specific comparison, the affiliate disclosure linked in the footer is where it would be noted.

We'd rather have fewer, deeper categories

It would be easy to split this directory into fifteen or twenty narrow categories to look more thorough, but a category with two comparisons in it isn't actually useful to anyone browsing — it just adds another click. We've kept the list at seven deliberately, and we'll only add an eighth, or split an existing one, once there's enough real content behind it to justify the extra click for readers rather than just padding out this page.

If you came here from a search engine

There's a decent chance you landed on this exact page looking for something specific — a particular product, a particular pairing — rather than browsing categories for their own sake. If that's the case, the fastest path is usually the search box on the all comparisons page, which checks titles and summaries across every category at once instead of making you guess which section a product belongs in. This page exists mainly for the opposite situation: when you know roughly what kind of product you're deciding on, but haven't settled on the two specific options yet. Bookmarking this page is also reasonable if you tend to make one or two significant purchases a year across different categories — a phone this spring, an appliance in the autumn — since the card descriptions above are written to still make sense on a second or third visit, months apart, without needing to re-read everything from scratch. Either way, nothing on this page requires a login or tracks your browsing to work — it's a plain directory, and it's meant to stay that way.
