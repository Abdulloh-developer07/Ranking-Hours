Photoshop vs Canva vs Figma

Three popular design tools that solve three different problems. Here is how to pick the one that fits the work you really do.

Last updated September 30, 2026

The short version

Photoshop, Canva and Figma are often put in the same list, but they were made to solve different problems. Photoshop edits images. Canva helps you make finished designs quickly from templates. Figma is for designing screens, like websites and apps, and for working on them with other people.

So the best way to choose is not to compare them feature by feature. Ask what you will make most often. Retouched photos, a flyer for your shop, or a mockup for an app each point to a different tool.

Many people end up using two of them, and that is normal. You do not need to pick one forever. A small business might use Canva every day and pay a photo editor who uses Photoshop for a special job.

A note on how we write: this guide uses public information and the makers' own documentation. We do not claim months of daily use of each tool, and we do not print prices, because plans and limits change often. Check the official pages before you pay.

What each tool is built for

Photoshop

Photoshop is an image editor from Adobe. It works with layers, masks and brushes, which means you can change one small part of a picture without touching the rest. People use it to retouch portraits, remove objects, combine several photos, fix colours and prepare images for print or the web. It can also make graphics and simple layouts, but that is not where it shines.

Canva

Canva is a browser-based design tool with an app version too. You begin with a template for a post, poster, menu, CV or presentation, change the words and pictures, and export. It also has a library of photos, icons and fonts, some free and some in paid plans. Its aim is to let people with no design training make something decent in minutes.

Figma

Figma is made for interface design. Designers use it to plan websites and apps, build buttons and menus, and link screens into a clickable prototype. Its biggest idea is teamwork: several people can work in the same file at the same time, and developers can look at the design and take measurements from it.

Ease of learning

Canva is the easiest to start. Most people can finish a first design within a few minutes, because the template does much of the thinking. The risk is that everything looks like a template if you do not change it.

Figma sits in the middle. The basics, like drawing shapes and adding text, are friendly. But real interface design needs ideas like components, spacing systems and auto layout, and those take time to learn. Plenty of free lessons exist, which helps.

Photoshop has the steepest climb. It is a deep program with many panels, tools and menus. You can do simple jobs quickly after a few tutorials, but advanced skills such as masking, blending and colour work take months of practice. That depth is also why professionals value it.

If you only have a weekend and a deadline, choose the tool that matches your skill today, not the one you wish you had. A simple design that is finished beats a clever idea that is not.

Photos and image editing

If your work is about photographs, Photoshop is in a different class. It handles fine retouching, complex cut-outs, colour correction and layered effects. Newer versions also include tools powered by machine learning for things like filling in or removing parts of a picture. These speed up many tasks, though results still need a human eye.

Canva has basic photo tools: crop, filters, brightness, and a background remover in its paid plans. That is enough for social posts and simple edits. It will not replace a proper editor for portrait work or product photos that need detailed fixes.

Figma is not a photo editor. You can place images in a design and apply simple effects, but you would edit the photo elsewhere first.

A practical tip: if you need both a polished photo and a quick layout, edit the picture in Photoshop or another image editor, then place it in Canva or Figma for the final design.

Posters, posts and everyday design

For a poster, a social post, a menu or a school presentation, Canva is usually the fastest route. The sizes are already set for common platforms, the fonts pair well, and you can resize a design for another format in a few clicks. For a person running a shop or a small page, that saves hours every week.

Photoshop can make the same things, but you start from a blank canvas. That gives you more control and also more work. It makes sense when you have a specific vision or you are already comfortable in the program.

Figma can do posters too, and some designers do use it for that. But it is not built around print or social templates, so you would be doing more by hand.

One thing to watch in Canva is sameness. Because many people use the same templates, designs can look alike. Change the colours, swap the fonts, and use your own photos to make it yours.

Website and app design

This is Figma's home ground. You can design every screen, keep a shared set of buttons and colours, and test how screens connect. When a developer joins, they can open the same file, see sizes and colours, and export images. This is much smoother than sending flat pictures back and forth.

Photoshop was used for web design for many years, and some people still do. But it was not made for this job, and most teams have moved to tools built for interfaces.

Canva can make a simple website or a one-page layout in some plans, which suits a small personal page. It is not a tool for designing a custom app or a large site.

If you plan to build a website from a template platform, you may not need any of these. Many site builders have their own design tools. Use a separate design program when you need more control than the builder gives you.

Working with others

Figma was built for teamwork from the start. People can edit together, leave comments, and see each other's cursors. Changes appear live, and old versions are kept.

Canva also supports sharing and team features. You can invite people, comment, and keep a brand kit with logos, colours and fonts so everyone uses the same style. For small teams that make lots of posts, this is a big help.

Photoshop files are usually passed around as files, though Adobe offers cloud features for sharing and review. It works, but it feels more like handing over a document than editing the same one together.

If you are a team of one, collaboration may not matter. If you work with a client, who must give feedback and approve, check how easily they can comment without needing a paid account.

Cost and plans

We do not list prices here because they change and differ between countries. What we can explain is how each one tends to work.

  • Photoshop is sold by subscription, either on its own or as part of a larger Adobe bundle. If you stop paying, you lose access to the program.
  • Canva has a free plan that is useful for simple work. Paid plans add more templates, stock content, brand tools and extra features. Team plans cost more again.
  • Figma has a free plan with limits, and paid plans for professionals and teams. The limits mostly affect how many projects or editors you can have.

Before you pay, find out which features you really need. Many people buy a plan for one feature, such as a background remover or a brand kit, and then use it twice. Try the free version for a real project first. Also check whether your school or employer already gives you access to any of them.

Computers and devices

Photoshop is a desktop program and needs a reasonably capable computer. Large files with many layers can slow down a weak machine, so check the official system requirements and consider how much memory you have. It also has a version for tablets, with fewer features.

Canva and Figma run in the browser, so they ask less of your hardware. This is one reason both are popular on budget laptops and school computers. Both have desktop and phone apps, though the full experience is on a bigger screen.

Exporting and keeping your work

Think about the day you want to move your work somewhere else.

All three can export common formats like PNG, JPEG and PDF. Photoshop has its own layered file format, which keeps all your layers, and other programs can open it with varying success. Figma files live in the cloud and can be exported as images or PDF. Canva designs can be downloaded in several formats, but editable files are held inside Canva.

Whichever you use, keep a finished copy outside the tool: a PDF for documents and a PNG for images. Also save your original photos in a separate folder. Then if a plan changes or an account has a problem, your work is not stuck.

Fonts and licences are another detail. A font that is free in one program may not be free to use in print or in a logo. If you are making something for a client, read the licence for any stock image, icon or font you use.

Who should choose what

Photoshop is the right pick if you

  • edit photographs or do detailed retouching
  • need full control over layers, masks and effects
  • already use other Adobe programs and want them to work together
  • work as a photographer, retoucher or digital artist

Canva is the right pick if you

  • make posts, posters, menus and slides regularly
  • have no design training and want results fast
  • need a team to keep one consistent brand look
  • work on a budget or start with a free plan

Figma is the right pick if you

  • design websites or apps
  • work with developers or other designers
  • need prototypes that show how screens link together
  • want one shared file instead of many versions

Print or screen?

Where your design ends up changes the tool you need and the settings you pick.

For screens, such as websites, social posts and slides, you work in pixels and a colour mode called RGB. Almost every free or paid tool handles this well. For print, such as flyers, business cards and banners, you need more care. Print uses a different colour mode called CMYK, a higher resolution, and often extra space around the edges called bleed so the cut does not leave a white line.

Photoshop gives you the most control over print settings. Canva can export a print-ready PDF in its paid plans, and it is good enough for many local print shops. Figma is not made for print at all.

If you are printing something for the first time, talk to the print shop before you design. Ask for the size, the file type and the bleed they want. Five minutes on the phone can save you a reprint.

Colours look different on different screens

The colours you see on your monitor will not match every other screen or a printed page. That is normal. If colour accuracy matters, for example a brand logo, order a small test print first and compare it in daylight.

Fonts, colours and a consistent look

Good design is often less about the tool and more about a few simple choices made carefully.

Use no more than two or three fonts. One for headings and one for body text is plenty. Pick colours from a small set, maybe one main colour, one accent and two neutrals. Then repeat them everywhere. When people see the same colours and fonts again and again, they start to remember you.

Canva's brand kit and Figma's shared styles are built for this. You set your colours and fonts once, and anyone on the team can use them. In Photoshop you can save swatches and text styles, but it needs a little more setup.

Leave space. Beginners often fill every gap on the page. Empty space is not wasted. It gives the eye a place to rest and makes the important part stand out.

Make text easy to read. Dark text on a light background, or the other way round, is easiest. Be careful when you put words over a busy photo. If you cannot read it quickly on a phone screen, a visitor will not try.

Smart features and automatic tools

All three tools now include features that use machine learning to save time. Photoshop can fill, extend or remove parts of an image. Canva offers tools that can suggest layouts, write short text or remove backgrounds. Figma has added helpers for naming layers and tidying up designs.

These can be very useful, especially for repetitive jobs. But keep a few cautions in mind. The results can look generic, and sometimes they get small details wrong, like hands, text in the picture or logos. Always look closely at the finished image. Also check the rules for commercial use, because the terms for generated content may differ from the terms for your own work.

Features in this area change quickly and some are limited to certain plans or regions. Look at the maker's current page before you rely on one.

A simple way to learn

If you are new, you do not need a long course. A small, steady plan works better.

Week one

Pick one tool. Make three small designs that you will actually use, like a post, a poster and a simple card. Copy a design you like, then change it until it becomes your own.

Week two

Learn the basics of alignment, spacing and text size. Most beginner mistakes come from these, not from missing features. Redo your first three designs with what you learned and compare them.

Week three and beyond

Add one new skill at a time. In Canva, that might be building your own brand kit. In Figma, it could be making a button that you reuse across screens. In Photoshop, start with layers and masks, because they are the foundation for almost everything else.

Ask someone for honest feedback. A friend who sees your design for five seconds will tell you what is not clear, and that is more useful than another tutorial.

Questions to ask before you commit

Take a minute with these before you start a paid plan.

  • What will I make in the next three months? Name the actual projects.
  • Who needs to open or edit my files, and which tool can they use?
  • Does the free plan already cover most of this?
  • Can I cancel easily, and will I still be able to open my files after I do?
  • Do I need this every week, or only for one big project?

If most answers are short and clear, you are ready to choose. If not, try the free version for a week and come back to the list.

Examples from real work

A cafe owner

The owner needs a menu, a weekly post and an Instagram story. Canva handles all of it, and the brand kit keeps colours and logo the same. For a new menu photo shoot, they hire a photographer who edits in Photoshop and sends back finished pictures.

A student building a portfolio

The student plans a personal website. Figma is the right place to sketch the pages and test the flow. The photos in the portfolio are first cleaned up in a free or paid image editor, then placed in the design.

A freelance photographer

Photoshop is the main tool, because the work is about images. Canva might still be useful for making quick price lists and social announcements.

A small start-up

The team uses Figma for the product screens and Canva for marketing posts. No one on the team opens Photoshop, because they have no photo work. This is a good example of why using two tools is often better than one tool that does everything badly.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Picking the most powerful tool by default. A heavy program slows you down if your job is simple.
  • Using a free plan for client work without reading the limits. Some content is only allowed in paid plans, and licences differ.
  • Not saving a copy outside the tool. Keep exports and original files in a folder you control.
  • Copying templates without changing them. Your design will look like everyone else's.
  • Learning three tools at once. Pick one, learn it well, and add another only when you have a clear reason.

Common questions

Is Canva good enough for professional work?

For many business tasks, yes, such as social media, simple print pieces and presentations. For detailed image editing, unusual layouts or full brand systems, designers often use more advanced tools.

Can Figma replace Photoshop?

Not for photo editing. Figma is for interface and layout work. The two do different jobs and often work well together.

Do I need to pay for any of them?

Not always. Canva and Figma have free plans that are useful for learning and small projects. Photoshop is subscription-based, though trial periods are usually available. Try each on a real task first.

Which one should a complete beginner start with?

Start with Canva if you want to make something quickly. Start with Figma if you want to design websites or apps. Choose Photoshop if your main goal is editing photos.

Are there free alternatives?

Yes. There are free image editors and free design tools, and some are very good. They may have fewer features or a different workflow, so test them on a real project before you commit.
