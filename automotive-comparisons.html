Car Comparisons and Buying Guide

What to ask before you buy a car, from running costs to used-car checks, with links to our full comparisons of popular models.

Last updated September 30, 2026

Start here

A car is the second biggest purchase most families make, after a home, and unlike a home it loses value every year. That is why a bad choice hurts more than it does with a phone or a laptop. You pay for it every month through fuel, insurance, repairs and the money it loses just by getting older.

This page is the starting point for our car guides. It explains the questions to ask before you look at any model, the main body types and engine types, what really matters for safety, how to think about running costs, and how to avoid the common traps of buying a used car. At the end it links to our detailed comparisons.

A note on how we work. Our guides are based on published specifications, official documents and public information. We do not road-test every car, and we do not make up driving figures. Prices, equipment lists and rules about import, tax and registration change often and differ by market, so we do not print them. Please check the exact version of any car, and the current rules, before you pay.

Six questions before you look at cars

1. What is the car for?

Be honest about the usual week. A short city commute, long highway trips, a family with children, a business with deliveries and a rough country road all call for different cars. Write the typical week down, and then add the unusual trips that happen a few times a year.

2. How many people and how much luggage?

Count seats you really use, and think about child seats, which take more space than people expect. Check the boot with your own luggage, a pram or sports gear.

3. What is the full budget?

The price of the car is only the start. Add insurance, annual taxes and fees, fuel or charging, servicing, tyres and parking. Then add a reserve for repairs, especially for a used car.

4. Where will it be parked and driven?

A long car is hard in a narrow courtyard. A low car scrapes on bad roads. A big SUV is stressful in a crowded city. Think about garages, gates, speed bumps and unpaved sections.

5. Who will fix it?

Spare parts and good mechanics are a big part of ownership. A popular model with plenty of parts and workshops is easier to live with than a rare one.

6. How long will you keep it?

If you change cars every three years, resale value matters a lot. If you drive one until it falls apart, reliability and repair costs matter more.

Add these up over the number of years you plan to own the car, and divide by the distance. This gives you a cost per kilometre, which is a better way to compare cars than the sticker price. A cheaper car with high fuel use and poor resale can end up costing more than a pricier one that is efficient and holds its value.

We avoid giving figures, because prices change quickly and differ between sellers, but the method works whatever the numbers are.

Body types

  • Sedan. A classic family car with a separate boot. Usually comfortable and stable on highways. Loading tall items is harder.
  • Hatchback. Compact, easy to park and flexible, since the rear seats fold down. Good for city use.
  • Crossover and SUV. A higher seating position, more ground clearance and a roomy feel. Often more expensive and heavier, and they use more fuel than a similar sedan.
  • Minivan and MPV. The best choice for large families and many bags, with flexible seating and sliding doors. Less sporty and bulkier.
  • Pickup. Strong and practical for work and rough roads, but big, thirsty and less comfortable for daily city use.
  • Station wagon. A long loading space with car-like handling, less common in some markets.

A higher car is not automatically safer, and a bigger one is not automatically better. Think about your roads and parking before you fall for the look.

Engines and fuels

Petrol

The most common choice. Simple, widely serviced, and available at every station. Fuel use depends heavily on engine size, car weight and driving style.

Gas conversions

In Uzbekistan, many drivers use methane or propane, either from the factory or from a fitted conversion, because the fuel is cheaper per kilometre. This can save money, but it takes some boot space for the tank, may slightly reduce power and needs good installation and regular checks. A poor conversion can be unsafe, so use an approved workshop, and check that the car's papers show the change.

Diesel

Efficient on long trips and good for towing, but less common in some markets and sensitive to fuel quality. Check local availability before choosing one.

Hybrid

A petrol engine with an electric motor and a small battery. It saves fuel, especially in city traffic, and needs no plug. It costs more at first, and long-term battery health matters when buying used.

Plug-in hybrid

A larger battery that you charge, so short trips can be electric, with a petrol engine for long ones. It makes sense only if you can charge regularly. Otherwise you are carrying a heavy battery for nothing.

Electric

Quiet, smooth and cheap per kilometre to run, with fewer moving parts to service. The key questions are range, charging and what happens in your climate. We look at this in more detail below.

Electric cars: what to know

Electric cars are growing quickly, and our comparisons of Tesla and BYD Atto and of BMW i4 and Tesla Model 3 look at popular examples. Here is the practical side.

Range

The official range comes from standard tests and is higher than most people get in real life. Highway speed, cold weather, hot weather with air conditioning, heavy loads and hills all reduce it. Treat the official number as the best case and plan with a margin.

Charging at home

Most owners do the bulk of charging at home overnight. If you live in a flat without a private parking place, think carefully. A reliable place to charge is the biggest factor in how easy electric ownership will be. Have a qualified electrician check the supply and install a proper charger.

Public charging

Public chargers are expanding, but they are not yet everywhere, and speeds and prices vary. Before buying, map the routes you drive and see where chargers are, whether they are working and what connectors they use.

Battery

The battery is the most valuable part. Check the battery warranty, which usually covers a number of years or kilometres. For a used electric car, ask for a battery health report.

Rules and incentives

Import duties, taxes, registration rules and charging incentives for electric cars have changed from time to time, and may change again. Check the current rules with official sources before you buy, and be careful with sellers who promise benefits without proof.

Automatic or manual

Automatics are now the default for many drivers. They are easier in traffic and less tiring. Manuals are often cheaper, can be simpler to repair and give more control, but they need more effort in the city.

Automatic gearboxes come in several types. A conventional automatic uses a torque converter and is generally smooth and durable. A CVT, or continuously variable transmission, is smooth and efficient but feels different, and some designs are sensitive to poor maintenance. A dual-clutch gearbox is quick and efficient but can be jerky at low speed, and some are costly to repair. Electric cars use a simple single-speed gear.

Whatever type you consider, ask about the service interval for the gearbox oil, and read owner feedback on the specific model and gearbox. A well-maintained gearbox of a reliable type can last for years. A neglected one can fail very expensively.

Safety: what really matters

Safety is one place to avoid trade-offs. The most important points are:

  • Airbags. Front, side and curtain airbags protect in different crashes. Check how many the version you are looking at has, since cheaper versions may have fewer.
  • Stability control. Helps prevent skids. It is standard on most modern cars, but check older ones.
  • Anti-lock brakes. Keep steering control while braking hard.
  • Seat belts and child seat anchors. Check that all seats have belts, and that child seat anchors suit your seats.
  • Crash test ratings. Independent organisations publish results. Ratings apply to the tested version and the tested market, and the standards change, so compare only like with like.
  • Driver aids. Automatic emergency braking, lane-keeping and blind-spot warning can help, but they are aids, not replacements for careful driving.

Also look at tyres and brakes, which are the most important parts that touch the road. Good tyres are among the best safety upgrades you can buy.

Comfort, space and everyday use

These are easy to overlook in the showroom and annoying every day afterwards.

  • Sit in every seat, including the back. Check headroom, legroom and how easy it is to get in and out.
  • Check the driver's position, the view and the mirrors. Can you see out well?
  • Test the air conditioning, because summers are hot, and the heating in winter.
  • Listen for wind and road noise at highway speed.
  • Check how the infotainment works, and whether your phone connects easily.
  • Look at the boot opening, the load height and the spare wheel.

Try parking it. A car that is difficult to park will make you unhappy every day, while one with good cameras and sensors can save you many scratches.

Local assembly, imports and parts

The Uzbekistan market has a strong local manufacturing base for popular models, and a wide range of imported cars as well. Local models often have an advantage in parts supply, repair knowledge and running costs. Imported cars can offer more technology, different designs and a different feel, but check parts and service closely.

Whichever you pick, ask these questions. Is there an authorised dealer and service centre in my city? How quickly do parts arrive? How much do common parts, like brake pads, filters and bumpers, cost? Are there independent workshops that know this model? Is the car sold with a local warranty, and does it cover what I expect?

For imported or used cars, check the import papers, the customs documents and the registration carefully. Buy only from sellers who can show clear documents.

Buying a used car

A used car can be excellent value, but it is where most expensive mistakes happen. Follow a calm process.

  • Check the papers. Compare the VIN on the car, the documents and the registration. Make sure the seller is the real owner. Check for fines, liens and restrictions with the official authorities.
  • Ask for the history. Service records, previous owners and accident history help. Be careful if the seller cannot explain gaps.
  • Inspect the body. Look for uneven gaps, differing paint shades and signs of repair. A paint thickness gauge can show repainted panels.
  • Look underneath and under the bonnet. Rust, leaks and damaged parts are warning signs.
  • Check the odometer. Compare the reading with the condition of the pedals, seats and steering wheel. Odometer tampering exists.
  • Test drive. Drive at different speeds, on rough roads and with the air conditioning on. Listen for knocks, vibrations and noises.
  • Get a professional inspection. An independent mechanic is worth the fee.
  • Negotiate and write it down. Agree on the price and any repairs in writing.

If the price looks too good to be true, ask why. A trusted dealer with a warranty costs more, but it also takes away much of the risk.

Financing and payment

Many buyers use instalments or a bank loan. This is not financial advice, but a few cautions apply to everyone.

Read the whole contract, including the interest rate, the fees, the insurance requirements, the penalties for late payment and the rules for early repayment. Calculate the total amount you will pay, not just the monthly payment. Check that the monthly payment is comfortable even if your income dips. Insurance, fuel and maintenance still have to be paid on top.

Be cautious about long loans on cars that lose value fast. You may owe more than the car is worth. And never sign anything you do not understand.

Looking after your car

Regular care is far cheaper than repairs. Follow the service schedule in the manual, and use the oil and parts recommended by the maker. Check tyre pressure monthly, and look at the tread. Watch the fluid levels. Replace the brake pads and discs before they are worn out.

In hot summers, check the cooling system and the battery, as heat is hard on both. In cold winters, check the battery, the tyres and the antifreeze. Keep the car clean to protect the paint, and fix small rust spots early.

Keep all receipts. A full service record helps you with warranty and raises the resale price.

A better test drive

A five-minute loop around the block tells you almost nothing. Ask for at least thirty minutes, and plan the route.

  • Start the car cold if possible. Hard starting, smoke or odd noises are easier to notice before it warms up.
  • Drive in city traffic, on a highway and on a rough road, if you have access to all three.
  • Brake firmly in a safe place. The car should stop straight, without pulling or vibrating.
  • Let go of the wheel briefly on a straight, empty road to see if the car drifts.
  • Try the gearbox in all modes, including reverse, and notice any delay or jerk.
  • Switch on the air conditioning, lights, wipers, windows and every control.
  • Park it, reverse it and check the visibility.

Take someone with you who is calm and does not feel pressure to buy, and have them ask the questions you forget.

Insurance and paperwork

Before you drive away, make sure the paperwork is right. This protects you and makes selling the car later much easier.

Check that the registration is transferred correctly, that the documents match the car and that any required insurance is in place. Ask your insurer which optional cover makes sense for your situation, and compare quotes from more than one company. The price of insurance depends on the car, the driver and the area, so get a quote before you decide on a model.

Keep the originals of all documents in a safe place, and carry only what the law requires. Photograph the key documents and keep copies. If you buy through a dealer, ask for a written contract listing the car, the price, the warranty and what has been repaired.

Hot summers and cold winters

Climate changes how a car behaves.

In hot weather, the cooling system, the air conditioning and the battery work hard. Tyres wear faster and pressure rises. Dark interiors become very hot. Sunshades, tinted windows and parking in shade help. Check the coolant level before long summer trips, and do not leave children or pets in a parked car, even for a few minutes.

In cold weather, batteries struggle, tyres lose grip and fluids thicken. Use the right oil and antifreeze, check the battery before winter and think about winter tyres if you drive on ice or snow. For electric cars, cold reduces range noticeably, so allow a larger margin and pre-heat the car while it is plugged in.

Dusty roads clog air filters faster, so check them more often if you drive on unpaved routes.

Selling it later

Think about the end when you start. Cars that sell easily share a few traits: a popular model, a clean history, full service records and a good condition. Neutral colours are easier to sell than unusual ones. Cars with accident history or unclear papers lose much more.

When the time comes, clean the car well, fix small problems, take good photos and be honest in the advert. Show the documents. Meet buyers in a safe public place, and let them have the car checked. Be careful with payment, and complete the transfer officially before handing over the keys.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying with your heart on the first visit. Sleep on it, and compare at least three cars.
  • Ignoring running costs. Fuel and repairs add up fast.
  • Skipping the inspection. A small fee can prevent a huge loss.
  • Trusting the odometer and the seller's story. Verify them.
  • Forgetting parts and service. A great car with no support is a burden.
  • Stretching the budget. A car you can barely afford becomes a daily worry.

Common questions

New or used?

New cars give a warranty and the latest safety and fewer risks, but they lose value fastest. A well-checked used car can be great value. Decide by budget and how much risk you accept.

Is an electric car right for me?

It can be, if you can charge at home or at work, and your usual trips fit within the real range. Otherwise a petrol or hybrid car may be easier today.

Are gas conversions safe?

They can be, when done properly by an approved workshop with proper parts and regular checks. A poor installation can be dangerous.

Which brand is the most reliable?

Brands have reputations, but reliability varies by model, engine, gearbox and age. Look at owner feedback for the exact car.

How much should I save for repairs?

It depends on the car's age and condition. Keep a reserve, and the older the car, the larger it should be.
