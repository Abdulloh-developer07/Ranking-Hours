Microsoft 365 vs Google Workspace

Two office suites that do almost the same job in different ways. A plain guide to choosing the one that fits your work and your team.

Last updated September 30, 2026

Before we start

Most people do not choose an office suite. They inherit one. A school hands you a Google account, an employer installs Microsoft Word, or a friend sends a spreadsheet and you open it with whatever you have. This guide is for the moment when you actually get to decide, either for yourself, your family or a small team.

The honest summary is simple. Microsoft 365 is the stronger choice when your work needs deep spreadsheets, careful page layout or files that must look exactly right on every computer. Google Workspace is the easier choice when your team is spread out, works mostly online and cares more about sharing and speed than about fine control.

Neither one is a mistake. Both are used by millions of businesses, and both can read and save each other's files. The trick is to find which one will cause your team fewer small annoyances over the next few years.

A word about how this page is written. We rely on public information, the makers' own documentation and what people publicly report. We have not run a company on each suite and we do not claim test results we do not have. We also leave out prices, because plans, bundles and limits change often and differ by country. Look at the official pages on the day you buy.

What you are really buying

The names are a little confusing, so let us clear them up first.

Microsoft 365 is a bundle of apps and services. The best known are Word for documents, Excel for spreadsheets and PowerPoint for presentations. Outlook handles email and calendar, OneDrive stores files, and Teams covers chat and meetings. You can use the apps as installed programs on a computer or in a web browser, and the installed versions are more powerful.

Google Workspace is also a bundle. Docs, Sheets and Slides are the counterparts to Word, Excel and PowerPoint. Gmail and Calendar handle mail and schedules, Drive stores files, and Meet is for video calls. Almost everything runs in the browser, with a mobile app for phones.

The key idea is where the work lives. In Microsoft's world, the strongest tools are programs on your own computer, with the cloud as a helper. In Google's world, the cloud is the main place, and your computer is just a window onto it. That one difference explains most of the pros and cons that follow.

There is also a free side to both. A regular Google account gives you Docs, Sheets and Slides in the browser at no cost, with storage shared between Gmail, Drive and Photos. Microsoft offers free web versions of its main apps with a personal account, though the free storage is smaller. Free versions are good for learning and small tasks. Paid plans add business email on your own domain, bigger storage, admin tools and support.

Writing documents

Word is a very mature program. It handles long reports, books, contracts and anything with heavy formatting. Page numbers, tables of contents, footnotes, tracked changes and complicated styles all work the way professionals expect. If your document will be printed, sent to a lawyer or published, Word gives you the most control.

Google Docs is lighter. It is fast to open, very clear to use and easy to share. Typing, commenting and suggesting edits feel natural, and several people can write in the same file without stepping on each other. For notes, drafts, meeting minutes, school essays and team documents, it is often all you need.

Where it gets tricky is layout. A Word file with complex tables, text boxes and unusual fonts can look slightly different in Docs. The reverse is also true. If a document must look exactly the same on every device, send a PDF instead of an editable file.

Both programs now include helpers that can suggest text, summarise a page or fix grammar. These features differ by plan and by country, and they change quickly. Treat them as a first draft that you must read carefully.

Spreadsheets: the biggest gap

If you only remember one thing from this page, remember this. Excel is the more powerful spreadsheet program, and the difference matters most for people who work with numbers all day.

Excel can handle very large tables, advanced formulas, pivot tables, data connections, charts with fine control and automation through macros. Finance teams, analysts and engineers often build whole processes inside it. If someone says "we need Excel", they usually mean one of those features.

Google Sheets covers the everyday needs well: sums, charts, filters, simple pivot tables, conditional colours and a long list of formulas. It also has a few things Excel handles less gracefully, such as pulling data from another sheet that lives in the cloud, and sharing a live sheet with a team. It can be extended with small scripts, which clever users use to automate chores.

Size is where the limits show. Very large datasets, many formulas, or heavy calculations can slow a browser-based sheet down. Excel on a good computer will usually stay smoother. Both have official limits on cells and rows, so check them if your data is big.

For a family budget, a school project, a small shop's stock list or a team task tracker, Sheets is perfectly good. For financial models, big data cleaning and anything that must follow strict rules, Excel is the safer pick.

Presentations

PowerPoint gives you more design control, more animation options and a bigger library of templates. If you give talks often, work with a design team or need a polished result, it is hard to beat.

Google Slides is simpler. It has fewer tools, which for many people is a good thing. You can build a clean deck fast, share a link, and let others add slides. For classroom use, team updates and quick pitches, it is easy and reliable.

The question to ask is where you will present. If you will stand in a room with an unknown computer and a weak internet connection, a PowerPoint file saved locally is safer. If you will present from your own laptop or on a video call, either one works.

Always keep a PDF copy of an important presentation. Fonts, videos and animations can break when you move a file to another machine.

Email, calendar and meetings

Email is a big reason many teams pick one suite or the other. Gmail is known for fast search, clean threading and a simple layout. Outlook is known for rich organising tools, rules, shared mailboxes and strong links to the calendar. Both support business addresses on your own domain with a paid plan.

Calendars in both are good at scheduling meetings and checking when colleagues are free. Google Calendar feels lighter. Outlook's calendar is deeper, especially inside large organisations.

For video meetings, Microsoft uses Teams and Google uses Meet. Meet is simple to join from a link in the browser. Teams does more, with chat channels, file sharing and many add-ons, but some people find it heavier. If your team already uses a different tool for calls, check how well it works with your suite before you decide.

A small tip. Whichever you choose, set up your calendar with working hours and a time zone first. It prevents more scheduling confusion than any feature.

Working together

Google built its apps around sharing, and it shows. You press one button, send a link, and others can view, comment or edit. There is no attachment to lose and no "which version is final?" email. Changes appear as people type, and the history shows who changed what.

Microsoft has caught up in many ways. Files stored in OneDrive or SharePoint can be edited by several people at the same time, in the web apps and in the installed apps. Comments and sharing links work well. The experience can feel a bit more complicated, because you have to know where a file is stored and who has permission.

For a small team of five or ten who just want to share work, Google's approach is usually quicker to learn. For a company with many departments, strict permissions and compliance rules, Microsoft's tools often fit better.

Whichever you pick, agree on simple rules. Where do files live? Who owns each folder? How do we name documents? A tidy habit beats any feature.

Files, formats and getting out

Both suites open the other's formats. Google Docs can import a Word file, and Word can open a file downloaded from Docs. Most of the time, plain text, pictures and simple tables survive the trip well.

The trouble is in the details: unusual fonts, complex tables, macros, special formatting, and advanced spreadsheet functions. If you swap files between the two worlds often, test a few real documents before you commit. Open them in both and look carefully.

Also think about leaving. If you ever want to switch, can you export everything? Google lets you download your data in common formats. Microsoft files are in widely used formats too. Do an export test once a year, and keep a separate backup of the files you cannot afford to lose.

And remember the free alternatives. Programs like LibreOffice can open and save the common formats, which is useful as a backup option or for people who do not want a subscription.

Working offline

This sounds minor until the internet goes down at a bad moment.

The installed Microsoft apps work fully offline. You can write, calculate and present on a plane or in a village with no signal, and the files sync when you reconnect. This is a real advantage if your connection is not reliable.

Google's apps can also work offline, but you have to set it up in advance, usually with the Chrome browser and an offline setting turned on. Then recent files are available without a connection. It works, but you must remember to prepare, and not every feature is available offline.

If your internet is stable and you are mostly in an office, this barely matters. If you travel often or live somewhere with an unreliable connection, give it real weight.

Security, admin and privacy

For a one-person business, security means a strong password, two-step sign-in and careful sharing. Both suites support two-step sign-in. Turn it on first, on every account.

For a bigger team, the admin tools matter. Both give an administrator control over who can sign in, what can be shared outside the company, and how to remove a person who leaves. Microsoft's tools are deep and suit large organisations. Google's admin console is simpler and quicker to learn for small teams.

Think about sharing links. The most common leak is not a hacker, it is a document shared with "anyone with the link" and then forgotten. Review your sharing settings every few months, and prefer sharing with named people.

On privacy, read each company's own policy and your plan's terms, especially if you handle client data or health or legal records. Rules about where data is stored and how it is used can differ by plan and region. If you work in a regulated field, ask your adviser before you move.

Phones and tablets

Both suites have mobile apps for reading, light editing and commenting. Google's mobile apps are simple and fast, and many people do real work on a phone with them. Microsoft's apps are capable too, and Word, Excel and PowerPoint also exist in a combined app.

Editing on a small screen is still awkward for long documents or big spreadsheets. Use your phone to check, comment and fix small things, and save the heavy work for a bigger screen.

If your team uses Android phones, Google's apps are built in and feel natural. On iPhone, both suites work well. If you are choosing a new computer along with the suite, see Windows 11 vs macOS and our laptop comparisons.

If you decide to switch later

Teams change suites more often than you might think, usually when a bill grows or a new manager arrives. A move is easier when you plan it like a small project.

Start with a list of what lives where: documents, spreadsheets, shared folders, email archives, calendars and any scripts or macros. Mark the items that are important and the ones nobody has opened in two years. You can leave the second group behind.

Move a small pilot group first, maybe two or three people. Let them use the new suite for two weeks and write down every problem. Typical trouble spots are complex spreadsheets, shared calendars and email rules. Fix those before you move everyone.

Then set a clear date, tell people early, and give them a one-page guide. Keep the old accounts available in read-only mode for a while, so no one panics when they cannot find an old file.

The people side matters more than the tools. Most complaints after a switch are not about missing features. They are about habits, like where the shortcut button went. Be patient and answer questions kindly.

Habits that make any suite work

A good set of habits is worth more than the logo on the box. These small rules work in both worlds.

  • Give every file a clear name with a date or version in it, like "price-list-2026-09".
  • Keep one folder tree for the team and agree who can change it.
  • Write comments instead of making silent changes when you edit someone else's work.
  • Close the loop. When a comment is resolved, mark it so people know.
  • Archive old projects once a year so search stays useful.
  • Make a copy before a big change, so you can go back.

Also teach new team members one thing on day one: where to find the latest version of everything. It sounds obvious, and it prevents most of the confusion that makes people blame the software.

Which one fits which team

A freelancer or very small business

Either works. Choose the one your clients use, so you can swap files without trouble. If you have no preference, start with the free Google tools and upgrade only when you need business email or more storage.

A school or a study group

Google's tools are simple to set up and easy for students to share. Many schools already offer them. If your teacher requires Word files, use Word.

A growing company with a finance team

Microsoft's suite usually wins, mainly because of Excel and the deeper admin tools. Many accounting and reporting systems also connect easily to Excel.

A remote team of creatives or writers

Google is often a natural fit. Writers and editors comment on each other's drafts, and nobody has to install anything. Add a design tool as needed. See Photoshop vs Canva vs Figma for that part.

A family

Whatever is free and already on your phones. Shared lists, a simple budget sheet and a photo folder cover most family needs without paying anything.

How to test before you pay

Spend one week on a real trial, not a demo.

Take three real files your team uses: a long document, a big spreadsheet and a presentation. Open them in the suite you are considering. Look for broken layouts, missing fonts and slow formulas. Ask a client or colleague to open them as well.

Then run one small project fully inside the tool. Write a report together with a colleague, comment on it, and finish it. Notice how much time you spend asking "where is that file?". If the answer is often, think about how files are organised.

Finally, check support and billing. Can you add and remove people easily? Can you cancel without a fight? Is it clear who pays for what? These dull questions decide whether you stay happy for years.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing for features you will never use. Most people use a tiny part of any suite. Pick the one that does your daily tasks well.
  • Mixing suites without a reason. Half of the team in one, half in the other creates a mess of attachments and copies.
  • Skipping backups. Cloud storage is not a backup. A deleted file can disappear for everyone at once.
  • Forgetting two-step sign-in. Most account problems start with a reused password.
  • Ignoring licence rules. Home and student plans may not allow business use. Read the terms.
  • Not training people. A short guide with three rules about files and sharing helps more than a long manual.

Common questions

Can I use both Microsoft and Google tools?

Yes, and many people do. A common pattern is to use one as the main suite and open the other when someone sends a file. Just avoid splitting a single team across both without a clear reason.

Is Google Docs good enough for Word users?

For most everyday writing, yes. If you rely on advanced layout, templates or special formatting, you may miss Word's tools. Try your real documents first.

Which is cheaper?

It depends on the plan, the number of users and what you need. Free versions exist for both. Compare the exact plans on the official pages for your country, and check what is included, like business email and storage.

Does Excel really beat Google Sheets?

For advanced analysis, large data and complex models, usually yes. For everyday sheets and shared lists, Google Sheets is simpler and often enough.

Do I need an internet connection?

Microsoft's installed apps work fully offline. Google's apps need setup for offline use and are mostly built for being online.
