Adobe Premiere Pro vs DaVinci Resolve

Two serious video editors with very different habits. Here is how they differ in cost, colour, speed and teamwork, and which one fits your work.

Last updated September 30, 2026

The short version

Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve can both edit a full film, a YouTube channel or a wedding video. Neither one is "the pro tool" and the other "the toy". Both are used on real, paid projects every day.

The difference shows up in how you work and how you pay. Premiere Pro is a subscription and fits neatly with other Adobe apps. DaVinci Resolve has a free version that is surprisingly full, plus a paid Studio version that is bought once. Resolve is also well known for its colour tools. Premiere is well known for being everywhere, so tutorials, templates and freelancers who know it are easy to find.

If you want one quick rule: choose Premiere if you live inside Adobe or work with teams that do. Choose Resolve if you want to start without paying, care a lot about colour, or dislike subscriptions.

A note on our method. This page is based on public information, the makers' documentation and published requirements. We do not claim to have edited a feature film in both, and we do not print prices because plans change. Please check the official pages before you buy.

Two different families

Adobe Premiere Pro belongs to Creative Cloud, a group of apps that includes After Effects for motion graphics, Audition for sound, Photoshop for images and Illustrator for drawings. The apps are built to pass work back and forth. You can edit a clip in Premiere, send it to After Effects for an animated title, and see the change appear without exporting a file.

DaVinci Resolve comes from Blackmagic Design, a company that also makes cameras and video hardware. Instead of separate apps, Resolve puts several jobs into one program. It has pages for importing media, quick cutting, full editing, visual effects, colour, audio and exporting. You move between pages rather than between programs.

This is the biggest difference in feel. Adobe gives you a set of tools that connect. Resolve gives you one big workshop with different rooms.

Cost and how you pay

Premiere Pro is sold by subscription. You can pay for Premiere alone or for a bundle of Adobe apps. As long as you pay, you get the latest version and updates. If you stop, you lose access.

DaVinci Resolve has a free version, and a Studio version that is paid once. The free one is a full editor, not a short demo. Studio adds extra features, such as more advanced noise reduction, some effects, support for more formats and higher resolution outputs.

What does that mean in real life? If you edit for fun, for school or for a small channel, the free version of Resolve may be all you ever need. If you earn money from editing, the cost of either one is small compared with your income, so pick by workflow and not by price.

Do the sum over several years. A subscription for four years costs far more than a single purchase, but you also get updates and the other Adobe apps. A one-time purchase is cheaper, but check what the maker says about future upgrades. We leave out actual prices, so check the official pages for your country.

Learning curve

Premiere Pro follows the classic editing layout: a media bin, a preview monitor and a timeline. If you have ever used other editors, it feels familiar. There is a huge amount of free teaching material, from beginner to advanced, so any question you have has probably been answered.

Resolve has a different layout. The page system is easy to follow once you understand it, but it can look crowded at first. The Cut page is made for fast editing, and the Edit page works like a traditional timeline. Many beginners start with the Cut page, then move to Edit as they grow.

Colour and effects are where each one asks the most from you. Resolve's colour page is deep, with nodes that let you build a look step by step. It is powerful but takes time. Premiere's colour tools are friendlier, and for many projects they are enough.

Whatever you choose, plan a few weeks before you feel quick. Start with a small project, like a one-minute clip, and finish it fully, including export. Finishing teaches more than watching ten lessons.

Editing itself

For the day-to-day work of cutting clips, trimming, adding titles and fixing audio levels, both programs do the job well. You drag clips to a timeline, cut them, and add transitions and music.

Premiere Pro has a long history and a very flexible timeline. It has useful shortcuts, a sensible way to handle nested sequences, and features like text-based editing, where you cut video by editing a transcript of what was said. Its tools for working in a team and across projects are mature.

Resolve's editing is solid and sometimes faster for simple projects, especially with the Cut page. Some editors like how it handles multiple cameras and how quickly it shows changes. Others prefer the habits they built in Premiere. Neither is wrong.

The honest answer is that editing speed depends far more on your skill and your keyboard shortcuts than on the program. Pick the one you can learn comfortably and stick to it for a few projects before you judge.

Colour and visual effects

Resolve earned its fame in colour. It began as a colour grading tool used in film and television, and later grew into a full editor. Its colour page gives you precise control over skin tones, contrast, secondary colours and the look of a whole scene. If your footage is shot in a flat profile and needs a careful grade, it is one of the best tools for the job.

Resolve also includes Fusion, a node-based area for visual effects and motion graphics. It is deep and can do much, though it has a steep learning curve.

Premiere has colour tools built into its panel, which are easy to use and good for most social and business video. For heavy visual effects and animation, you turn to After Effects. This works well but means opening another program, and another part of the subscription.

If you do a lot of grading, Resolve is a natural fit. If you do a lot of motion graphics, the Adobe route is common. If you do neither, both will do fine.

Audio

Good sound matters more than most beginners expect. Viewers forgive a slightly soft picture but they leave when the audio is bad.

Premiere has basic audio tools and links to Audition for deeper work like cleaning noise and mixing. Resolve has Fairlight, a full audio workspace built in, with mixing, effects and tools for dialogue. For a solo creator, having it all in one program is handy.

For simple videos, a good microphone and careful level setting beat any plug-in. If your recordings are noisy, fix the room and the microphone first. Software can reduce noise, but it cannot create clarity that was never recorded. Our guides on podcast gear and home studio setups cover this side.

Speed, hardware and stability

Video editing is one of the heaviest things a computer does. Large files, high resolution and many layers can slow even strong machines. Both programs use the graphics card and processor heavily, and both ask for a decent amount of memory.

Resolve is often described as GPU hungry, so a good graphics card helps a lot. Premiere also benefits from a strong GPU and fast storage. Always check each maker's official system requirements for your exact version, because they change.

A few things help on either program. Use a fast SSD for your active project. Keep your media on a drive with space to spare. Use proxies, which are lighter copies of your footage, if your computer struggles with big files. And close other heavy programs while you edit.

About stability, both can crash at times, as any big program can. Save often, use autosave, and keep backups of your project files and media. Do not rely on one drive for anything you care about.

Formats, cameras and exports

Both programs open most common video files. But formats from certain cameras and phones, like some compressed 10-bit files, can be slower or need extra support on certain systems. If you shoot with a specific camera, check how it behaves in the program before you buy.

Blackmagic's own cameras work especially well with Resolve, as you might expect. Many other cameras also work smoothly. If you are looking at gear, our camera guide may help.

For export, both give you ready presets for YouTube, social platforms and common formats. The free version of Resolve has some limits on the highest resolutions and certain codecs. Studio removes more of them. If you export at very high resolution or in unusual formats, read the feature list carefully.

Keep a master file. Export one high-quality version and store it, then make smaller versions from that. It saves you from re-rendering the whole project later.

Working with a team or clients

If you work with other editors, the program they use matters more than your own preference. Many agencies and freelancers use Premiere, so sharing project files with them is easier. This is a real advantage when you hire help or take on a client's existing project.

Resolve has its own team features, including shared projects through a database, and it is used in professional studios too. But sharing between Resolve and Premiere is not smooth. You will usually export formats like XML or EDL, and some effects will not carry over.

Clients can also affect your choice. Some ask for specific project files, and others only want the final video. Ask early, because it is much easier than rebuilding a project.

Keeping a project tidy

Messy projects are the real reason many edits go slowly. Whichever program you use, a few habits will save hours.

Make one folder for every project. Inside it, keep separate folders for video, audio, music, graphics and exports. Name files clearly. A clip called "interview_anna_take2" is much easier to find than one called "MVI_0487". Do this before you import anything.

Use bins or folders inside the program in the same way. Put interviews in one place and cutaway shots in another. Label your best takes with a colour or a marker so you can find them fast.

Save versions. Instead of overwriting one file, save "project_v1", "project_v2" and so on at the end of each work session. If something breaks or a client changes their mind, you can go back.

Both programs can relink files that moved, but it is easier to avoid moving them. Do not rename folders in the middle of a project.

Your first week

Do not try to learn every button. In week one, learn how to import clips, cut on the timeline, add a title, adjust sound levels and export. That is already a complete video. Everything else can wait until you need it.

Making video for different platforms

A video for a phone feed is not the same as a video for a TV screen. Before you start, decide where it will be watched.

For vertical platforms, the frame is tall and narrow, and the main subject should sit in the middle where it will not be covered by buttons and text. For YouTube and most websites, the frame is wide. For a presentation in a room, make sure the text is large and the audio is clear on speakers.

Both Premiere and Resolve let you create a project in a custom size, and both can reframe a wide video for a tall one. Do it deliberately and check the result, because automatic reframing can cut off faces or titles.

Captions matter more than many people think. A large share of viewers watch with the sound off, especially on phones. Add subtitles to anything meant for social media. Premiere has a text-based workflow that can create captions from speech, and Resolve also has tools for subtitles. Always read the generated text, since names and technical terms are often wrong.

Finally, check your export on the real device. Watch the finished video on a phone, a laptop and, if you can, a TV. Problems with brightness, sound and text size show up fast.

Free versus paid in Resolve

Many people wonder whether the paid Studio version is worth it. The honest answer is that most beginners and hobby editors do not need it at first.

The free version already gives you the full editing, colour and audio workspace. Studio adds extras that matter in certain jobs: stronger noise reduction for dark footage, some advanced effects, tools that work with more formats, and the ability to work at higher resolutions and use more than one graphics card.

A good way to decide is to work in the free version until something stops you. If you hit a wall, you will know exactly which feature you are missing, and you can decide if it is worth the price. Buying early "just in case" is the most common waste.

If you do pay, check what the licence covers, such as how many computers you can activate and whether future major versions are included. Read the details on Blackmagic's official page.

Subscription or buy once: how to think about it

This choice is about more than money.

A subscription lowers the cost of starting. You pay a small amount each month, and you always get the newest features and bug fixes. It also tends to include extras like cloud storage and fonts. The downside is that it never ends. If your income drops, you still have to pay, or you lose access to the program and, in some cases, to the ability to edit your old projects.

A one-time purchase asks for more at the start. After that, you own that version. For someone who edits steadily for years, it is usually cheaper. The downside is that big new versions may cost extra, and you might wait for features that subscribers already have.

Ask yourself three questions. How many years will I edit? Will I need other apps from the same company? And would I be stressed about a monthly bill during a quiet season? Your honest answers usually point to one side.

Who should pick which

Premiere Pro suits you if you

  • already use Photoshop, After Effects or other Adobe apps
  • work with teams or clients who use Adobe projects
  • want the biggest amount of tutorials, templates and plug-ins
  • are comfortable with a subscription

DaVinci Resolve suits you if you

  • want to start free without a time limit
  • care about colour grading
  • prefer buying once instead of renting
  • like having editing, effects, colour and audio in one place
  • have a strong computer, especially a good graphics card

If you want both, nothing stops you. Some editors cut in Premiere and grade in Resolve. It adds steps, but for projects where colour matters it can be worth it.

Examples of how people choose

A student making a short film

Money is tight and the computer is decent. Resolve's free version is the obvious start. The student learns editing, colour and audio in one program and pays nothing.

A YouTuber who posts weekly

Speed and templates matter. Premiere with its large library of tutorials and ready-made titles is comfortable. A creator who is also comfortable with Resolve may like its fast Cut page.

A small agency

The team already pays for Adobe and shares assets across Photoshop and After Effects. Premiere fits into the workflow, and clients send Adobe files.

A wedding videographer

The footage needs good colour and a quick turnaround. Either works. The decision usually comes down to the editor's habits and computer.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Switching tools in the middle of a project. Finish the current job, then try the new tool on the next one.
  • Judging by one tutorial. Many videos online are made by fans of one program. Test both on your own footage.
  • Ignoring your computer. The best software is useless if it stutters. Check the requirements.
  • Not backing up. Keep copies of the original clips and the project files on separate drives.
  • Chasing features you will not use. Most videos need cuts, titles, sound and a simple colour fix.

Common questions

Is the free version of DaVinci Resolve really free?

Yes, the free version has no time limit. It does have some limits compared with Studio, such as some effects and format support, so check the current feature list on Blackmagic's page.

Is Premiere Pro better for beginners?

Not necessarily. Premiere has more beginner lessons online, which helps. Resolve's Cut page is friendly too. The best beginner tool is the one that runs well on your computer and that you enjoy using.

Can I switch later?

Yes. Editing skills transfer from one program to another, though shortcuts and layouts change. Keep your original footage and exports, and you can move without losing work.

Do I need a powerful computer?

For simple HD videos, a modern mid-range machine is often fine. For 4K, many layers, or heavy effects, you need a stronger processor, more memory and a good graphics card.

Which one do professionals use?

Both. You will find each in professional work. Some studios use Premiere, some use Resolve, and some use both. Pick based on your job, not on someone else's choice.
