Camera comparisons: choosing a system around how you actually shoot

A practical look at sensor size, autofocus, video capability and lens ecosystems, built around published specifications and the trade-offs that show up once a camera leaves the shop.

Focus: sensors, autofocus, video, lenses Best for: first system purchase or an upgrade decision Reviewed: published specifications and hands-on sample galleries

Buying a camera is one of the few electronics purchases where the box you take home is really just the entry point to a much larger system. The body matters, but so do the lenses you'll add over the next few years, the memory cards, the extra battery you'll eventually need, and the software you'll use to get through a memory card full of raw files. A comparison that only looks at megapixels and burst speed misses most of what actually decides whether someone is happy with a camera six months in.

This guide isn't a ranking of "best cameras." It's a walk through the specifications that genuinely change the experience of using a camera, and the ones that mostly exist to fill out a spec sheet. Along the way we'll point out where two cameras that look similar on paper can behave very differently once you're holding one in low light, chasing a moving subject, or trying to pull focus during a video clip.

Start with the kind of photography you actually do

Every camera comparison should begin with an honest answer to one question: what will this camera spend most of its time photographing? A camera bought for family trips and a camera bought for paid portrait sessions can come from completely different parts of a manufacturer's lineup, even if they cost about the same.

Travel and everyday photography tend to reward a smaller, lighter body with a versatile zoom lens more than they reward the highest resolution sensor available. You're going to be carrying this thing on your shoulder for a full day, and a camera that stays in the bag because it's heavy isn't taking any pictures at all. Portrait and event work shifts the priority toward lens selection, reliable eye-detection autofocus, and a body that can shoot a burst of frames without the buffer filling up mid-sequence. Wildlife and sports photography lean hard on autofocus tracking and telephoto lens reach, categories where the gap between an entry-level and a higher-tier autofocus system is genuinely obvious in the field, not just on a spec sheet. Video-first buyers — vloggers, run-and-gun shooters, anyone building a YouTube channel — care about a completely different list: rolling shutter behaviour, overheating limits, a flip-out screen, and clean audio input options.

None of this means a single camera can't do more than one of these jobs well. Many hybrid mirrorless bodies handle stills and video competently. But when two cameras are close in price and a comparison has to break the tie, the deciding factor is usually which of these use cases the camera was actually built around.

Sensor size and what it actually changes

Sensor size gets treated as the headline spec in almost every camera comparison, and it's a reasonable place to start, but it explains less than the marketing copy implies. The three formats you'll run into most often are full-frame (roughly 36 by 24mm), APS-C (roughly 23.5 by 15.6mm, though the exact figure varies slightly by brand), and Micro Four Thirds (17.3 by 13mm). A larger sensor, all else being equal, gathers more light per pixel, which shows up as cleaner detail at high ISO and a shallower depth of field at a given aperture — useful for portraits, less useful if you're trying to keep a whole landscape in focus.

What sensor size does not automatically deliver is a better photo. Lens quality, the photographer's exposure and composition choices, and how the files are processed afterward all move the needle more than the format printed on the spec sheet. A well-handled APS-C camera with a good lens will regularly outperform a full-frame body paired with a cheap kit zoom. Sensor size is a real factor, particularly for low light and background separation, but it's one input among several rather than a shortcut to a final answer.

"The sensor sets the ceiling. The lens, the light, and the person pressing the shutter decide how close you get to it."

Micro Four Thirds deserves a specific mention because it's often dismissed too quickly in comparisons that only look at sensor area. The smaller sensor means smaller, lighter lenses for an equivalent field of view, which matters enormously for anyone carrying a full kit of lenses rather than one body and one lens. For travel and for video work where a compact, stabilised kit outweighs the last stop of low-light performance, the trade-off can be a genuinely reasonable one, not just a budget compromise.

Autofocus: the spec that matters more than it gets credit for

If sensor size is the headline spec, autofocus is the one that actually determines how many usable photos come out of a shoot. Modern cameras advertise subject-detection autofocus for people, animals, birds, and vehicles, and the difference between a system that reliably locks onto and tracks an eye through a crowd and one that hunts and drifts is enormous in practice — far bigger than the difference in resolution between two comparable bodies.

A few things are worth checking specifically rather than trusting a single marketing claim. How does the camera behave in dim, low-contrast light, such as an indoor venue with mixed lighting? How quickly does it re-acquire a subject after something briefly blocks the frame? Does eye-detection hold steady when the subject turns their head, or does focus jump to the background? Sample photo and video galleries shot in real conditions, rather than a bright, high-contrast test chart, tell you far more about autofocus than the headline number of focus points a camera claims to have.

For video specifically, autofocus needs to be evaluated separately from stills autofocus, because the two don't always perform the same way on the same camera. Smooth, confident focus pulls during a video clip, without visible hunting or focus breathing, are a distinct capability that some cameras handle far better than their stills autofocus scores would suggest.

Video considerations for hybrid shooters

Cameras aimed at photographers who also shoot video need to be judged on a different checklist than the one used for pure stills bodies. Resolution and frame rate numbers on a spec sheet — 4K at 60 frames per second, for instance — only tell part of the story, because how a camera actually delivers that footage varies a lot between models.

Rolling shutter

Most mirrorless cameras use an electronic shutter for video, which reads the sensor line by line rather than all at once. If that readout is slow, fast pans or quick movement in the frame produce a visible skew, sometimes called the "jello effect." This is rarely listed as a spec, but it shows up clearly in real footage and is worth checking through sample clips before assuming two cameras are equivalent for video.

Recording limits and heat

Continuous recording time, and whether a camera stops itself to cool down during long clips, matters a lot for anyone recording lectures, interviews, or long single takes. A camera that performs beautifully in a five-minute review sample can behave very differently after thirty continuous minutes in a warm room, so it's worth looking specifically for overheating reports on the exact model under consideration.

Codec, bit depth and audio

Higher bit-depth recording and flat colour profiles intended for grading matter mainly to people who plan to colour-grade their footage afterward; casual clips destined straight for social media rarely benefit from them. What does matter for almost everyone is audio: a standard microphone input, reliable in-camera stabilisation for handheld clips, and a screen that flips out to face the person recording, since these have a bigger day-to-day impact than most video specifications combined.

Lenses and mounts: the real long-term investment

The body is usually the smallest total expense in a camera system over a few years of ownership. Lenses, and the mount a body uses, are the decision that's hardest to reverse later, because switching brands later often means replacing the entire lens collection rather than just the camera body.

Before settling on a system, it's worth checking three things about the mount specifically: how many lenses the manufacturer itself offers at the focal lengths that matter for your work, whether reputable third-party lens makers support the mount (which usually brings prices down considerably), and how well older lenses from a previous system can be adapted, if that's relevant to your situation. A system with a thin native lens lineup can still be a poor long-term choice even if the camera body itself scores well in a comparison, simply because you'll eventually want a lens that doesn't exist yet, or exists only at a steep price.

Field note

Renting a lens for a weekend before buying it outright is a cheap way to confirm a focal length actually suits how you shoot — a surprising number of "must-have" lenses turn out to sit in the bag once the novelty wears off.

Low light and ISO performance, realistically

Every camera manufacturer publishes an eye-catching maximum ISO number, and almost none of that number is useful in practice. What matters is how much fine detail and colour accuracy survive at the ISO setting you'll realistically use — typically somewhere well below that advertised ceiling. Two cameras with the same maximum ISO figure can look meaningfully different once you view a real photo at full size rather than a thumbnail.

The most reliable way to judge low-light performance is to look at unedited sample images or video shot at a camera's mid-to-high ISO range, rather than the maximum extended setting used mainly for marketing. Lens aperture plays a large role here too — a slower kit zoom will struggle in dim venues regardless of how capable the sensor underneath it is, so a low-light comparison between two cameras is only fair if the lenses attached to them are roughly comparable in speed.

Stabilisation, ergonomics and the things you feel every day

In-body image stabilisation, usually shortened to IBIS, has become common across most mirrorless cameras, and it makes a genuine difference for handheld shooting in dim light or for smoothing out handheld video. How many stops of stabilisation a camera claims is a reasonable starting point, but the practical benefit depends heavily on the lens attached and the specific movement being corrected, so it's worth treating those numbers as a rough guide rather than a guarantee.

Ergonomics rarely appear on a spec sheet at all, yet they shape daily use more than almost anything else. Grip depth, button placement, whether a screen tilts or fully articulates, viewfinder resolution and eye relief, and weather sealing around the body all become part of the shooting experience within the first week of ownership. A camera that scores well on paper but feels awkward to hold for an extended session tends to get left at home, which makes every other spec on the sheet irrelevant.

Storage, connectivity and the unspectacular parts of ownership

The less glamorous specifications — card slot type, battery life rated in shots per charge, wireless transfer speed, and USB charging support — end up mattering a great deal once a camera is in regular use. Dual card slots matter for anyone shooting paid work who wants an automatic backup while shooting; a single slot is a reasonable compromise for casual use but a real risk for professional jobs. Battery life ratings are measured under standardised test conditions that rarely match real shooting habits, particularly for video or continuous autofocus tracking, so it's sensible to plan on a spare battery for a full day out regardless of the number printed on the box.

Matching a camera to the work: five common cases

01

Travel and everyday carry

Prioritise weight, a versatile zoom range, and a battery that survives a full day of sightseeing over maximum resolution or burst speed.

02

Portraits and events

Reliable eye-detection autofocus, a fast prime lens, and a comfortable burst rate for candid moments matter more than sensor size alone.

03

Wildlife and sports

Autofocus tracking speed and telephoto lens reach dominate the decision; a smaller sensor with a longer, faster lens often wins in the field.

04

Vlogging and run-and-gun video

A flip-out screen, dependable stabilisation, and a clean microphone input outweigh raw video resolution for most creators.

05

Studio and product work

Tethering support, colour accuracy, and resolution for large prints or crops matter more here than autofocus speed or burst rate.

Budget tiers and where money actually buys improvement

Entry-level mirrorless cameras have reached a point where autofocus and image quality are genuinely good for most everyday use, and the jump into a mid-tier body mainly buys faster burst shooting, a more advanced autofocus system, better weather sealing, and improved video specifications rather than a dramatically sharper image. The step from mid-tier into professional-grade bodies tends to show up mostly in build quality, dual card slots, faster data throughput, and autofocus performance under demanding conditions like fast sports or dim indoor venues — differences that matter enormously to someone shooting for a living and far less to someone photographing family events on weekends.

A practical way to allocate a camera budget is to spend proportionally more on the lens than first instinct suggests. A mid-range body paired with a genuinely good lens will usually produce better results than a top-tier body paired with a cheap kit zoom, and the lens will likely still be useful after the body is eventually replaced.

Buying used or refurbished

The used and refurbished market is one of the more overlooked ways to get meaningfully more camera for the same budget, particularly for lenses, which tend to hold their optical performance well over many years of use. For camera bodies specifically, it's worth asking about shutter actuation count where the camera uses a mechanical shutter, checking the sensor for dust spots or scratches in sample photos taken at a small aperture against a plain background, and confirming what warranty coverage, if any, transfers with the sale. Buying from a dealer offering even a short warranty is generally a safer route than a private sale for a first used-camera purchase, since it gives some recourse if a hidden fault shows up in the first few weeks.

Weather sealing, durability and how long a body lasts

Weather sealing gets mentioned in almost every camera comparison, and it's easy to overrate. Gaskets around buttons and card doors help a body survive light rain, dust on a beach, or a cold morning, which matters a lot for landscape and wildlife photographers who can't choose their conditions. For someone who mostly shoots indoors or on sunny weekends, it's a nice extra rather than a deciding factor. It's also worth knowing that "weather-resistant" isn't a standardised term, so two manufacturers can use it to describe very different levels of protection, and the lens you attach matters as much as the body, since an unsealed lens on a sealed body leaves a gap right at the front.

Longevity is a separate question. Most modern mirrorless bodies are rated for a shutter life in the hundreds of thousands of actuations, far more than the average hobbyist will ever use, and electronic shutters have no mechanical wear at all. What tends to age a camera faster than physical wear is software support: firmware updates stop, a newer model adds a feature you now want, and the lens mount you invested in starts receiving less attention. That's one more reason to look at how long a manufacturer supports its bodies before buying, not only at the specifications printed on the box today.

Common misconceptions worth retiring

A few ideas keep resurfacing in camera comparisons despite not holding up well in practice. More megapixels does not automatically mean a sharper photo; beyond a certain point, extra resolution mainly matters for large prints or heavy cropping, and can even work against low-light performance by packing more, smaller pixels onto the same sensor. Professional-grade gear does not produce professional-looking photos on its own — composition, light, and post-processing decisions matter more than which camera captured the raw file. And a higher price does not guarantee a better fit for a specific use case; a camera can be excellent and still be the wrong tool for the kind of photography someone actually wants to do.

Bottom line: camera comparisons age faster than most categories on this site, since firmware updates, lens availability, and pricing shift the practical picture regularly. Check current lens support and recent firmware notes for the exact model before deciding, rather than relying on a comparison written months earlier.

Frequently asked questions

Is a mirrorless camera always better than a DSLR?

Not automatically. Mirrorless bodies generally lead in autofocus tracking and video, and most new lens development is happening on mirrorless mounts, but a used DSLR system can still be excellent value for stills-focused photography on a tighter budget.

Do I need a full-frame sensor to take good photos?

No. Full-frame helps most with shallow depth of field and low-light detail, but APS-C and Micro Four Thirds systems produce excellent results and are usually lighter and less expensive to build a full lens kit around.

How much should I budget for lenses versus the camera body?

A rough starting point is to expect to spend at least as much on lenses as on the body over the life of a camera system, often more, since lenses tend to have a longer useful life than any single camera body.

A practical checklist before you buy

  • Define the two or three types of photography or video you'll shoot most often, and weigh the comparison toward those specifically.
  • Check autofocus performance through real sample galleries, not just the manufacturer's headline claims.
  • Confirm the lens mount has the specific focal lengths you need, at a price you're comfortable with, now or soon.
  • Look at low-light sample images at the ISO range you'll actually use, not the maximum advertised setting.
  • Hold the camera if at all possible — grip, button layout, and screen articulation matter more than they seem to on paper.
  • Budget for a spare battery, extra memory cards, and at least one additional lens from day one.
