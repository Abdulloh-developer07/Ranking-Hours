How We Evaluate Camera Sensors

Here's a plain breakdown of how we test resolution, color accuracy, and processing speed across consumer and professional cameras.

1. How Full-Frame Sensors Capture Light

A camera sensor is essentially a grid of millions of tiny light-sensitive points. When light hits one of these points, it generates a small electrical signal - and how efficiently a sensor converts light into signal (called quantum efficiency) is a big part of what makes one camera's image quality better than another's.

In our tests, we compare how sensor design affects real image quality across three reference cameras. Some sensors use a "back-illuminated" design that lets each pixel capture more light. Others prioritize faster data readout instead. We use a solid all-around performer as our baseline to keep comparisons fair and consistent.

We also test how well each camera's lens and sensor combination handles light falloff toward the edges of the frame - something that matters a lot for wide-angle and landscape photography.

Packing more megapixels into the same sensor size means each individual pixel gets smaller, which can affect how well the camera performs in low light or high-heat conditions during long shoots. We test this directly by monitoring how each camera performs during extended shooting sessions.

The Importance of Quantitative Sensor Metrics

Our advice: don't chase megapixel counts alone. Smaller pixels can actually reduce how much light each one can capture before clipping in tricky lighting - so a well-balanced sensor often beats a sensor that just has more megapixels.

We also check for optical filters that reduce moiré patterns (weird visual artifacts on fine repeating textures like fabric). Some cameras skip this filter for extra sharpness, others include it for cleaner results - we test both approaches with detailed test charts.

2. Dynamic Range and Noise Performance

Dynamic range is one of the most important things to look at in a camera - it's the gap between the darkest and brightest detail a sensor can capture in a single shot. As you raise the ISO to shoot in darker conditions, the sensor amplifies its signal, which also amplifies noise.

We measure this carefully across all three cameras. One camera stands out for handling low light especially well, keeping noise impressively low even when heavily underexposed. Another delivers very predictable, accurate exposure that's great for studio work. A third uses smart internal processing to reduce distortion in high-contrast scenes.

We publish our full testing methodology openly, including the exact charts, lenses, and software we use - so other reviewers and photographers can verify our results themselves rather than just taking our word for it.

Some newer sensors use "dual gain" technology, which switches to a more sensitive mode automatically once light drops below a certain level. This helps preserve shadow detail without blowing out highlights. We test exactly where each camera makes that switch, so you know how to expose your shots properly under mixed lighting.

We also test how heat affects image quality during long shoots - extended recording sessions can warm up the sensor slightly, which adds a bit of extra noise. We run 30-minute stress tests on each camera to see how well it manages this.

3. Processing Speed and Autofocus

Modern cameras process an enormous amount of data in real time - white balance, color correction, noise reduction, and autofocus all happen simultaneously the instant you press the shutter. How fast a camera can do this determines how many photos it can take in a row before slowing down.

We measure the exact delay between pressing the shutter and the photo being saved. One camera stands out here with a notably faster data pipeline, which reduces distortion during fast action shots. Another has excellent autofocus tracking, scanning the frame hundreds of times per second to keep a moving subject in focus. A third is a bit slower to read data but processes it with excellent precision.

We also test autofocus tracking under tough conditions - fast, unpredictable movement in low light - since that's where cheaper autofocus systems really start to struggle compared to higher-end ones.

Finally, we test how quickly each camera clears its memory buffer after a burst of shots, since a camera that locks up while saving files isn't much use during a critical moment. We test this across different memory card types so you know what to expect in real shooting situations.
