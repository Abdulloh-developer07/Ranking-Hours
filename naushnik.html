Sony WH-1000XM5 vs AirPods Max 2: two different ideas of what "premium" means

Sony builds around noise-cancellation engineering and codec flexibility. Apple builds around materials and ecosystem polish. Neither approach is wrong — the right pick depends on which parts of daily listening matter most to you.

Sony WH-1000XM5
Apple AirPods Max 2
Compared on: ANC, sound, comfort, battery, calls Based on: published specifications and independent reviews

Premium wireless headphones stopped being a simple Bluetooth-and-bass category a while ago. At the top end of the market, the decision comes down to a handful of specific trade-offs: how aggressively the headphones cancel outside noise, how good they sound with the music service you actually use, how comfortable they stay after three hours on a flight, and how deeply they expect you to be invested in one company's ecosystem. None of these trade-offs has a universally correct answer — they depend entirely on your own listening habits and the devices already sitting in your bag.

Sony and Apple approach that decision from different starting points. Sony has spent close to a decade refining noise cancellation and offers the flexibility to work well across Android and iOS alike. Apple builds the AirPods Max line around premium materials, a tightly integrated software experience, and features that only reach their full potential inside Apple's own ecosystem. Both are legitimate approaches — the better fit depends on which of those priorities lines up with how you'll actually use the headphones, and on how much of your daily technology already lives inside one company's products versus spread across several.

Noise cancellation: two different strengths

Both headphones cancel outside noise effectively enough to satisfy most travellers, commuters, and open-office workers, and independent measurements generally put them close together overall. Where they diverge is in which frequencies each handles best. Sony's system has historically shown a particular strength with steady, low-frequency drones — engine noise on a plane, the hum of an HVAC system, road noise in a car — the kind of constant background sound that ANC handles most convincingly.

Apple's approach, built around its own audio chip, leans toward handling trickier mid-range sounds, including voices and the kind of irregular chatter you'd hear in a coffee shop or open office. Where Apple genuinely stands apart is Transparency Mode: it pipes outside sound back into your ears with very little perceptible delay, to the point that it can feel like you're not wearing headphones at all. That's a real, noticeable advantage for anyone who needs to stay aware of their surroundings — running outdoors, keeping half an ear on a conversation at a desk, or simply not wanting to feel fully sealed off in public.

Comfort has a real effect on how "good" ANC feels day to day. A tight seal blocks more sound physically, but if the earcups start to ache after an hour, no amount of processing makes up for that — fit matters as much as the chip inside.

Sound quality and codec support

For listeners who care about squeezing the most detail out of a streaming service, wireless codec support genuinely affects what you hear. Sony supports LDAC, a high-bitrate Bluetooth codec capable of streaming up to roughly 990kbps on supported Android devices, which keeps lossless tracks from services like Tidal noticeably more detailed and better separated than a standard Bluetooth connection. Sony also includes DSEE Extreme, a processing feature that attempts to restore some detail to heavily compressed audio files in real time — useful for older MP3 collections, less relevant if your listening is already lossless.

Apple relies on AAC, which tops out at a considerably lower bitrate than LDAC and doesn't offer the same headroom for high-resolution streaming. What Apple's setup does well is consistency: custom drivers keep distortion low even at higher volumes, and the tuning out of the box favours a balanced sound with deep bass that doesn't overwhelm vocals — a safe, broadly likeable default for listeners who aren't planning to dig into an equaliser. For movies and supported video content, Apple's spatial audio with head tracking adds a genuine sense of three-dimensional space that a standard stereo mix can't replicate, a feature that matters a lot to some buyers and barely registers with others depending on how much video content factors into daily use.

Build quality and long-session comfort

Sony builds the WH-1000XM5 from recycled plastics and keeps total weight around 250 grams, which is genuinely light for headphones in this category. The headband adjusts smoothly, and soft, pressure-relieving earcup padding stays comfortable even with glasses on during long sessions. The trade-off is that the WH-1000XM5 doesn't fold flat, so packing it for travel means relying on the included case rather than collapsing it into a pocket-sized shape.

Apple goes for a more premium materials story — machined aluminium arms, a breathable knit-mesh headband, and magnetically attached earcups that make cleaning or swapping pads simple. That build quality comes with real weight: around 385 grams, noticeably heavier than the Sony, which some users report as contributing to neck fatigue during multi-hour sessions like long editing work or transcontinental flights. The exposed aluminium headband also shows scratches more readily than Sony's more understated plastic-and-fabric design.

Battery life and charging

Both headphones are built for a full day of typical use with ANC active, and neither requires the kind of anxious battery-watching that some earlier wireless headphones demanded. Quick-charge features on both brands can add several hours of playback from a short charging break — useful specifically for anyone who tends to forget to charge overnight and needs a fast top-up before heading out. Where it matters more is travel: a headphone that charges via USB-C and can be topped up from the same charger as a laptop or phone simplifies packing considerably compared to older headphones still using a separate charging standard, so it's worth confirming the charging port on whichever specific model you're considering.

Battery behaviour with ANC switched off versus on is also worth a mention. Both headphones last noticeably longer with active noise cancellation disabled, which is a reasonable option for a long flight where you'd rather stretch the battery through a full journey plus a connecting flight than risk running out with hours of travel still ahead. Neither company makes this trade-off obvious in their marketing, since the headline battery figure is almost always the ANC-on number, so it's worth treating that published figure as closer to a worst-case estimate than a guarantee under every listening condition.

Microphones and call quality

Both companies use multiple microphones with active wind and background-noise reduction for calls, and both perform well in typical indoor environments — a quiet room or office rarely reveals much difference between them. The gap widens in harder conditions: windy outdoor calls, a busy street, or a crowded train. Sony's beamforming array is tuned to isolate your voice from steady background noise like wind and traffic, while Apple's system leans on the same transparency-related processing that makes its ANC tuning distinctive, generally producing clear, natural-sounding calls without the slightly processed quality some competing headphones introduce.

Software, controls and ecosystem lock-in

Sony's companion app works across both Android and iOS, offering EQ customisation, ANC strength adjustment, and a "speak to chat" feature that automatically pauses playback when you start talking. It's a genuinely cross-platform experience, which matters if your household or workplace mixes Android and iPhone users, or if you expect to switch phone brands in the future without giving up your headphones' full feature set.

Apple's experience is built specifically around its own devices. Instant pairing, seamless switching between an iPhone, iPad and Mac signed into the same Apple ID, and spatial audio with head tracking all work best — in some cases, only work at all — within Apple's ecosystem. For someone fully invested in Apple hardware already, this integration can feel closer to magic than a feature list suggests; for someone who splits time between an Android phone and a Windows laptop, most of that integration simply doesn't apply, and the AirPods Max 2 functions as a more ordinary (if still well-built) pair of Bluetooth headphones.

If neither one fits your budget

Both of these sit at the premium end of the market, and that price isn't necessary for everyone. Mid-range over-ear headphones from the same two brands, and from competitors like Bose or Sennheiser, now offer genuinely capable ANC and solid comfort at meaningfully lower prices — the gap between flagship and mid-range ANC performance has narrowed considerably in recent years. If lossless codec support and ecosystem integration aren't priorities for you, it's worth comparing a mid-tier option before assuming a flagship purchase is necessary just to get dependable noise cancellation. A mid-range pair also makes sense as a lower-risk way to confirm you actually prefer over-ear headphones to earbuds before spending flagship money either way, since fit preference between the two styles is surprisingly personal and hard to predict from specs alone.

Multipoint connectivity and everyday switching

Multipoint lets a pair of headphones stay connected to two devices at once — a laptop and a phone, for instance — and switch audio between them automatically when a call comes in or a video starts playing on the other device. Sony has supported multipoint for several generations now, and it works reliably across Android and iOS alike, which matters a lot for anyone who spends the day moving between a work laptop and a personal phone without wanting to dig into Bluetooth settings every time. Apple's approach leans on its own device-switching system instead, which works exceptionally smoothly between devices signed into the same Apple ID but doesn't extend the same seamlessness to a non-Apple laptop or an Android phone sitting alongside an iPhone.

This is a genuinely practical distinction, not a minor footnote. Someone working across a Windows laptop and an Android phone will get more consistent multipoint behaviour from Sony's implementation. Someone living entirely inside Mac, iPhone and iPad will barely notice the difference, since Apple's automatic switching covers that exact scenario about as smoothly as multipoint can.

Firmware updates and long-term support

Both companies have a track record of shipping firmware updates that improve ANC tuning, fix connection bugs, and occasionally add new features to headphones already in owners' hands — a real point in favour of buying into either ecosystem rather than a smaller, less established audio brand. Sony's update cadence has historically focused heavily on ANC and codec-related refinements; Apple's updates tend to roll out alongside broader iOS and macOS releases, which means new headphone features sometimes arrive tied to a phone or computer software update rather than a standalone headphone app update. Neither pattern is inherently better, but it's worth knowing that Apple's updates can occasionally lag slightly behind a numbered iOS release before headphone-specific features become available.

Case, accessories and what's actually included

Sony includes a semi-rigid carrying case with the WH-1000XM5, along with a USB-C charging cable and a 3.5mm audio cable for wired listening when the battery runs out or when regulations require a wired connection on a flight. The headphones fold inward at the hinges for a more compact case, though as noted earlier they don't fold flat. Apple ships the AirPods Max 2 with its distinctive Smart Case, which puts the headphones into an ultra-low-power state when stored — convenient, though it has drawn some criticism for feeling underbuilt relative to the price of the headphones themselves and for not fully protecting the earcups from scuffs during transport.

Repairability and long-term ownership

For anyone planning to keep a pair of premium headphones for several years, replaceable parts matter more than it might seem at purchase time. Sony's earcup pads are user-replaceable, and the company sells official replacements directly, which extends the useful life of the headphones well past the point where padding would otherwise wear out or lose its shape. Apple's magnetically attached earcups are similarly simple to swap, which is a genuine advantage baked into the design — though third-party parts and battery replacement have historically required an authorised service visit rather than an at-home repair, a distinction worth knowing if long-term self-serviceability is a priority for you.

A brief note on sound signature preference

Beyond the measurable differences in codec support and driver quality, the two headphones simply sound different in ways that come down to personal taste as much as engineering. Sony's default tuning tends toward a slightly warmer, bass-forward signature that many listeners find engaging for electronic music, hip-hop and pop. Apple's tuning is more neutral and vocal-forward out of the box, which tends to suit acoustic music, podcasts and dialogue-heavy content particularly well. Both support some degree of EQ adjustment through their respective apps, so a strong initial preference for one sound signature over the other isn't necessarily final — but it's worth listening to both with your own regular music before assuming the spec sheet alone settles the question.

Common misconceptions worth retiring

A higher advertised decibel reduction for ANC doesn't automatically mean quieter real-world listening — frequency response matters as much as the peak number, since a headphone can excel at blocking one type of noise while doing less for another. Codec support is not purely academic either way: LDAC's higher bitrate genuinely matters for lossless streaming on Android, but it does nothing for someone who mostly listens to standard-quality streaming or podcasts, where the practical difference becomes hard to notice. And ecosystem integration is a real, measurable convenience for people fully inside that ecosystem — not marketing fluff — but it offers close to zero benefit to someone who splits their devices across platforms, so its value depends entirely on your own setup rather than being a universal advantage either headphone can claim. Weight numbers on a spec sheet are worth taking seriously too, not dismissing as trivial — 135 grams doesn't sound like much written down, but distributed across the top of your head for a six-hour flight, it's a difference plenty of reviewers and owners describe as very noticeable.

Bottom line: fit is the one factor here that a spec sheet cannot tell you. If at all possible, try a pair in person before buying — comfort over a long session matters as much as any driver or codec specification, and it's the detail most likely to decide whether you actually reach for these headphones every day.

Frequently asked questions

Which headphones have better noise cancellation?

They're close overall. Sony tends to handle low, steady noise like engine drone better, while Apple handles mid-range sounds like voices slightly better and offers a more natural transparency mode. Neither is a clear all-around winner, which is why matching ANC strength to the specific noise you deal with most often matters more than chasing a single headline number.

Do I need an Android phone to get the most from the Sony WH-1000XM5?

No, but LDAC's higher-bitrate streaming is an Android-specific advantage, since iPhones don't support the codec. iPhone users will still get strong ANC and sound quality, just without that particular streaming benefit.

Is the AirPods Max 2 worth it if I don't own other Apple devices?

It's a well-built pair of headphones on its own, but a large part of its value comes from seamless switching between Apple devices and spatial audio features that depend on the Apple ecosystem — value that mostly disappears without other Apple hardware.

Which one is better for frequent air travel?

Both work well on flights, but Sony's lighter weight and stronger performance against steady low-frequency engine noise make it a slightly more comfortable choice for long-haul flights specifically.

Can I use either pair with a Windows laptop?

Yes, both connect over standard Bluetooth to Windows laptops, though Apple's most distinctive ecosystem features — seamless device switching and spatial audio with head tracking — won't be available outside Apple hardware.

Practical checklist before buying

  • Try both in person if possible — comfort over a long session is the factor a spec sheet can't show you.
  • Check whether your phone platform supports the codec you care about (LDAC needs Android).
  • Think about how often you need Transparency Mode, not just ANC strength.
  • Consider how deep your existing ecosystem investment is before paying for integration features.
  • If budget is tight, compare a mid-range model from either brand before committing to the flagship price.
  • Ask about earcup replacement parts if you plan to keep the headphones for several years.
