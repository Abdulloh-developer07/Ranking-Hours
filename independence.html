Declaration of Editorial Independence

What keeps a manufacturer, retailer, or advertiser from deciding what this site says about their product — and what to do if you think that line has been crossed.

The core commitment

Ranking Hours keeps its editorial judgment carefully separate from any commercial relationships it holds. A manufacturer, retailer, advertiser, affiliate program, or any other commercial partner does not get to decide which product receives a favourable conclusion. That line is the entire reason this page exists in the first place, and it's worth stating as plainly and directly as possible before anything else on this page.

Independence doesn't mean every single article exists in some kind of commercial vacuum. Product availability, affiliate links, and advertising all coexist around an editorial website that has to pay for itself somehow. The important principle isn't that commerce is absent — it's that those relationships are disclosed openly and never become a reason to alter a factual comparison or hide a real limitation.

Criteria first

Recommendations follow the criteria explained in the article, decided before any commercial link is added.

Disclosed, not hidden

Affiliate relationships and advertising are named openly rather than buried in fine print.

Open to correction

An article changes when the evidence changes — consistency with an old version isn't a reason to leave an error in place.

Readers can flag it

A factual error, an undisclosed conflict, or a misleading claim can be reported directly and gets checked.

How a recommendation actually gets made

Recommendations are always based on the criteria explained in each specific article — battery life, build quality, running costs, whatever is relevant to that particular comparison. If one product is genuinely better for a particular use case, the article explains why, in enough detail that the reasoning can be checked. If the evidence is mixed, the conclusion says so instead of manufacturing a tidy verdict the research doesn't actually support.

Keeping commercial and editorial decisions separate

Commercial activity is a normal part of running a publishing website, but it stays distinct from the explanation presented to readers. Advertising space, affiliate links, and retailer relationships are commercial features layered on top of a comparison — the factual analysis itself isn't rewritten because a particular product or seller happens to be commercially important to us.

What this guarantees, specifically

A retailer paying a higher commission does not get a better review. An advertiser does not get a friendlier mention. A manufacturer does not get to preview or approve an article before it's published. If any of these things were happening, this page would be describing a policy we don't actually follow — which is exactly the kind of gap the Contact page exists to catch.

When mentioning a specific retailer is legitimate

There are real, non-commercial reasons an article might mention one retailer, manufacturer, or region over another. Availability differs by country, a warranty might only apply regionally, or a product might genuinely be sold through a limited number of channels. Those practical facts are described as what they are — logistics — rather than dressed up as evidence of product quality.

Room to be wrong, and to fix it

Editorial independence also requires room for correction, which sounds like the opposite of a firm declaration but genuinely isn't. If a specification changes after launch, a source turns out to be outdated, or a statement is found to be inaccurate, the article gets revised. A correction is always preferable to leaving a known error in place for the sake of matching an older, already-published version of the page.

This matters because independence that can't admit fault isn't independence — it's just a different kind of bias, one that protects the site's own past work instead of a commercial partner. We'd rather be corrected in public than be quietly, consistently wrong.

How to flag a problem

If you notice a possible conflict of interest, an undisclosed relationship, a factual error, or a conclusion that seems to bend toward a commercial partner rather than the evidence, the Contact page is where to raise it. Include the specific article, the specific claim, and — where you have one — a source, so it can actually be checked rather than argued about in the abstract.

What independence looks like in a specific comparison

Take a comparison between two robot vacuums, one from a brand with a generous affiliate program and one from a brand with none at all. Independence means the second brand can still win the comparison outright if its navigation, suction, and app reliability are genuinely better — and if it does win, the article says so plainly, commission or no commission.

It also means the first brand's flaws don't get softened because the affiliate relationship makes it commercially convenient to like that product. If its dustbin is small, or its app disconnects constantly, that goes in the article exactly as it would if there were no affiliate relationship at all. A reader comparing the two should be able to tell, from the writing alone, that the commercial relationship isn't visible in the conclusion — because it genuinely isn't there.

A check you can run yourself

You don't have to take our word for any of this. Pick a category — audio, say — and look at whether our highest-rated products are consistently the ones most likely to carry a commission, or whether the pattern looks more mixed. A genuinely independent site should show a fairly random relationship between commission size and rating, not a suspiciously tidy one.

What we ask of anyone writing for this site

Anyone contributing research or writing to a comparison — whether a member of the core editorial team or an outside contributor — works from the same standard: research the products, apply the stated criteria, and write the conclusion the evidence actually supports. Nobody involved in writing a comparison is told in advance which product "should" win, and nobody is asked to adjust a finished conclusion to favour a specific retailer or brand.

Where someone working on a comparison has a personal connection to one of the products involved — say, they've previously worked for the manufacturer, or hold shares in the company — that connection gets disclosed, and depending on how close it is, that person may step back from writing that specific comparison. This mirrors the conflict-of-interest principle in our Ethics Code, applied specifically to the question of commercial independence.

What independence doesn't mean

It doesn't mean we're hostile to the brands we cover, or that a negative-sounding conclusion is somehow more trustworthy than a positive one. A product that's genuinely excellent should be described that way, commission or no commission — independence isn't about defaulting to criticism, it's about the conclusion following the evidence in either direction.

It also doesn't mean every comparison arrives at a confident, clean-cut verdict. Sometimes the honest answer is that two products are close enough that the deciding factor should be something personal to the reader — a feature they specifically need, a price they've already budgeted for — rather than anything we can rank from the outside. Saying "these are close" is itself a form of independence: it resists the pull toward manufacturing a decisive-sounding conclusion just to make the article feel more useful than the evidence actually allows.

The relationship between independence and money

We don't pretend this site runs on goodwill alone. Display advertising and affiliate commissions fund the research time, hosting, and editorial work behind every comparison. Refusing all commercial relationships would mean refusing to publish at all, which wouldn't actually serve readers better — it would just mean no comparisons existed to read. The realistic goal isn't zero commercial involvement; it's commercial involvement that never touches the conclusion.

That distinction is easy to state and harder to build, so it's worth being specific about the mechanism rather than just the intention. A comparison's structure — the criteria it uses, the sources it cites, the conclusion it reaches — is settled during research and drafting, before any retailer link is added. The commercial layer is added afterward, pointing to wherever the already-decided recommendation happens to be sold. If a product has no active affiliate program at all, it's still included and rated exactly the same way; the absence of a link changes nothing about the analysis.

What happens when a brand disagrees with a conclusion

Manufacturers and retailers occasionally reach out when they're unhappy with how their product was compared. We treat these messages the same way we'd treat a reader's correction report: check the specific factual claim being disputed against the sources the article was built from, and correct it if the brand is right. What we don't do is adjust a conclusion simply because a company would prefer a more flattering one — a disagreement about the verdict, as opposed to a factual error, doesn't trigger a rewrite on its own.

This distinction matters because "the brand didn't like it" and "the brand found an error" are very different situations, and treating them the same way in either direction — ignoring all brand feedback, or caving to all of it — would both be failures of independence. The first ignores genuine information; the second hands editorial control to whoever complains loudest.

What we don't accept, as a matter of policy

Pay-for-placement

No brand can pay to have their product included in, or excluded from, a comparison.

Review approval rights

No manufacturer sees a draft or gets sign-off before an article about their product goes live.

Commission-weighted scoring

A rating is never adjusted based on which retailer's program pays more for that product.

Silent removal of criticism

A genuine downside doesn't get quietly edited out after a brand complains.

If you ever find evidence that one of these lines has actually been crossed on this site, that's exactly the kind of report the Contact page exists for, and it would be treated as a serious failure of this declaration rather than a minor style complaint.

Questions readers sometimes ask about this

If you're independent, why do you use affiliate links at all? Because the alternative funding models — a subscription paywall, or accepting sponsorships that would compromise independence far more directly — would either restrict access to the comparisons or introduce exactly the kind of pressure this page describes avoiding. Affiliate commissions, kept separate from the conclusion by the process described above, fund the work without requiring either compromise.

How do I know the process you describe is actually followed? You don't, purely from reading this page — that's an honest limitation of any disclosure. What you can do is check the pattern across comparisons: whether high-commission products consistently win, whether genuine flaws in popular products get mentioned, and whether categories with no commercial incentive (like cars) still receive the same depth of critical analysis as ones that do.

What if I find a comparison that looks biased? Report it through the Contact page with the specific claim you're questioning. A vague sense that something feels off is hard to act on; a specific factual disagreement, or a pattern across several articles, is something we can actually investigate and respond to.
