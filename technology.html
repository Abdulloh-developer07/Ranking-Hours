How to use this page

Technology is a huge subject, and buying advice for it often falls into two bad habits. One is a giant "best of" list that tells you nothing about your own situation. The other is a spec sheet with fifty numbers and no explanation of which ones matter. This page tries to sit between them. It gives you a map of the comparisons on the site, a short set of questions that apply to nearly every gadget, and a glossary that explains common terms without jargon.

The comparisons themselves go deeper. Each one looks at two or three products or product families, explains where they differ in daily use, and says who each one suits. They are written from published specifications, the warranty and update policies that companies state, independent measurements where they exist, and the experience that owners commonly report. We do not claim to have lab-tested everything, and the pages say where a number comes from a published source. If you want to know how we judge things, read the methodology page and the explanation of our star ratings.

A good way to use the site: pick the area below that matches what you are shopping for, read the one or two comparisons that fit, then come back to the five questions and check your shortlist against them. That usually takes less than an hour, and it saves a lot of regret.

Five questions before any purchase

These questions work for a phone, a vacuum, a pair of headphones or a piece of software. Write your answers down before you open a shop page.

1. What is the main job?

Say it in one sentence. "I need a laptop for writing and video calls." "I want earbuds for the train." "I need to edit short videos for social media." A clear job tells you which features count and which are decoration. If you cannot say the job in one sentence, you are not ready to compare products yet.

2. What do I already own?

Look at your phone, computer, TV, router and accessories. Your existing setup creates friction or saves money. A new pair of earbuds works best with the phone you have. A monitor needs a port your laptop has. A camera needs memory cards, and a lens mount, and a bag. Buying something that does not fit your current gear is the most common and most avoidable waste.

3. How long do I want it to last?

Two years and six years are very different plans. For a phone, check the update promise. For a laptop, check whether memory and storage can be upgraded and whether the battery can be replaced. For software, check whether you can still open your files if you stop paying. A product that suits a three-year plan can be a poor choice for a six-year plan.

4. What will it really cost?

Add the extras: a charger that is not in the box, a case, a memory card, a subscription, an adapter, installation. Then think about the cost per year of use. A more expensive product that lasts twice as long often costs less. We say more about this below.

5. Who will fix it?

Before you buy, find out what happens if it breaks. Is there an official service point in your country? How long do repairs take? Does the warranty cover the battery and the screen? In some places this question matters more than anything on the spec sheet, and it is the one most buyers skip.

What changes fast and what does not

Not all technology ages at the same speed, and knowing the difference helps you decide when to spend more and when to save.

Fast-changing parts. Processors and graphics chips improve every year, and the newest model is usually only a modest step above last year's. Camera software and AI features change even faster, and they often arrive as updates, so an older phone can gain some of them. Prices of new models drop quickly after launch, and last year's version often becomes the best deal.

Slow-changing parts. Screens, speakers, keyboards, build quality and battery chemistry change slowly. A good screen from three years ago still looks good. A comfortable keyboard stays comfortable. A well-made case lasts. When you spend money on the parts that do not age, the product keeps its value in your hands.

Standards that shift now and then. Connector types, Wi-Fi generations and video standards change every few years. Rules in some regions have pushed phones and many gadgets toward the same USB-C charging port, which makes cables and chargers easier to share. A new standard rarely makes an older product useless overnight, but it can make accessories awkward over time.

A practical rule: spend on the parts you touch and see every day, such as the screen, keyboard, comfort and battery, and be relaxed about the chip, unless your work really needs the speed.

Ecosystems and lock-in

Many tech brands build a family of products that work best together. Apple's phones, tablets, laptops, watches and earbuds share features that do not work as well with other brands. Samsung, Google and Microsoft have their own versions of the same idea. Inside an ecosystem, things are smoother: files move quickly, calls ring everywhere, a headset switches between devices on its own.

That convenience is real, and it has a price. The more you own inside one family, the harder it becomes to leave, because switching means replacing several things at once and losing some features. This is called lock-in, and it is not always bad. If you are happy with the family, lock-in is just convenience. If you are unsure, it is worth knowing about before you buy the second and third product.

A sensible approach is to choose your main device first, usually the phone or the computer, and then pick accessories that fit it. Where possible, favour products that use open standards, like Bluetooth, USB-C and common file formats, because they keep your options open. And remember that mixing brands is fine. Most people do it, and most things work well enough together.

When to buy

Most tech follows a rough calendar. Apple usually shows new iPhones in September. Google has recently launched its Pixel phones in late summer. Samsung's main Galaxy S phones tend to arrive in the first months of the year, and its foldables in the summer. Laptop makers announce a wave of models at the start of the year and again in autumn. Consoles and graphics cards come on their own schedule.

What this means for you is simple. When a new model arrives, the previous one usually gets cheaper, and the difference between the two is often small. If you do not need the newest features, buying the previous generation a few months after the launch is a classic way to save money. Seasonal sales, such as back-to-school offers and end-of-year events, can bring good prices too, though some discounts are smaller than the advertising suggests, so compare against the price history if you can.

There is one reason not to wait: if your current device is broken or unsafe, such as a phone that no longer gets security updates. Waiting for a better price is not worth risking your accounts.

And a note on endless waiting. There is always a better model coming in six months. If a product fits your needs and your budget today, buying it is a perfectly good decision.

The real cost of a gadget

The price on the shelf is only the start. A smarter question is how much the product costs per year of use. A phone that costs 800 and lasts four years costs about 200 a year. A phone that costs 500 and lasts two years costs 250 a year. These numbers are examples, but the idea holds: durability and support can matter more than the sticker price.

Add the extras that are easy to forget:

  • chargers, cables and adapters that are not in the box;
  • cases, screen protectors and bags;
  • memory cards, extra storage or a cloud plan;
  • subscriptions for software, online games or services;
  • installation or setup, for things like air conditioners and routers;
  • a repair or battery replacement later in life.

Then subtract what you can get back. Some brands keep their value when you sell, which lowers the true cost. Others lose value fast, which is bad when you sell and good when you buy used. A used or refurbished product from a seller with a warranty can be an excellent choice, especially for things like laptops and phones that last for years. Just check the battery health, the warranty terms and whether the product is locked to someone else's account.

A plain-English glossary

Spec sheets are full of terms that sound technical and mean something simple. Here are the ones that come up most often.

Refresh rate (Hz)How many times per second the screen updates. Higher numbers like 120 Hz look smoother when you scroll or play games.
NitsA measure of screen brightness. More nits help in sunlight.
OLED and LCDTwo screen types. OLED lights each pixel separately, which gives deep blacks. LCD uses a backlight and is usually cheaper.
RAMThe short-term working memory of a device. More RAM helps with many apps open at once.
SSDFast flash storage that has replaced older spinning drives. It makes devices start and open files quickly.
GPUThe graphics chip. It matters most for games, video editing and 3D work.
USB-CA connector shape. The shape alone does not tell you the speed, the charging power or whether it can output video.
ThunderboltA very fast connection that uses the USB-C shape. Useful for fast drives and multiple monitors.
Wi-Fi 6, 6E, 7Generations of Wi-Fi. You need both a device and a router that support it to get the benefit.
CodecThe way Bluetooth audio is compressed. Your phone and headphones both need to support the same good one.
ANCActive noise cancelling. Microphones and electronics reduce steady sounds like engines.
IP68A rating for dust and water protection. It covers accidents, not heavy use in water, and it usually does not cover water damage on warranty.
mAh and WhUnits of battery size. Wh is easier to compare across devices, because it accounts for voltage.
Watts (W)The power of a charger or a device. Fast charging needs both a strong charger and a phone that accepts it.
Update policyHow many years a maker promises new software and security fixes. A longer promise means a longer useful life.

Buying in smaller markets

Many readers of this site live in countries where the biggest tech brands do not run a full local operation. That does not stop you from buying good products, but it adds some checks.

  • Find out who sells and supports it. An official local importer or distributor usually gives you a real warranty. An unofficial seller may only give you a shop guarantee, and repairs can be slow or impossible.
  • Check the market version. The same model name can have different network bands, plugs, features and languages in different countries. For phones, check that the bands match your operator, and for chargers check the plug.
  • Ask about services. Some features, such as contactless payments, voice assistants, call tools and certain AI functions, depend on the country and the language. Confirm that the ones you need work where you live.
  • Think about payments. Subscriptions and online stores often charge in dollars. Local bank cards do not always work, so check how you will pay before you rely on a paid service.
  • Protect against power problems. Where voltage swings or outages are common, use a surge protector for expensive items like laptops, TVs and air conditioners.
  • Keep your paperwork. Save the invoice, the serial number and the warranty card. They are your proof if anything goes wrong.
  • Be careful with very low prices. A price far below the market can mean a used unit, an older version, a grey import or a fake. Ask where the stock comes from.
  • Avoid cracked software. It can carry malware and put your accounts at risk. Many tools have free plans, student discounts or legal free alternatives.

Warning signs in reviews and adverts

Good buying advice is rarer than it looks, and some of what you read online is closer to advertising. Here are signs to watch for, on this site and anywhere else.

  • Only praise. Every product has a catch. A review with no downsides is probably not telling you the whole story.
  • No stated source. If a number has no source or method, treat it with care. Good pages tell you whether a figure is from the maker, from a lab or from experience.
  • Words like "best ever" and "must buy". They signal selling, not explaining.
  • No date. Prices and models change. An undated page may describe a product that no longer exists.
  • A winner for everyone. No product suits every buyer. Good comparisons say who each one is for.
  • Pressure. Countdown timers and "only three left" messages are tools to make you hurry. Take your time.
  • Hidden commercial links. Pages with affiliate links should say so. Ours are explained in the affiliate disclosure.

Questions people ask

Where should I start if I do not know what to buy?

Write down the main job in one sentence, then open the area that matches it from the map above. Read the comparison for the two options you are closest to, then check your choice against the five questions.

Should I buy the newest model?

Only if it has a feature you will really use. Last year's model, bought after a new one is launched, is often the better value.

Is a more expensive product always better?

No. Extra money buys better materials, more features or longer support, but only some of that may matter to you. Compare what you actually use.

Are refurbished products a good idea?

Often yes, if the seller gives a warranty and you check the battery health and account lock. They can save a lot on phones and laptops.

How do I know if a review is honest?

Look for stated sources, named downsides, a date and a clear explanation of who the product suits. Be careful with pages that only praise.

Do you test every product?

No. Our comparisons rest on published specifications, maker policies, independent measurements and owner reports, and we say when a number comes from another source. See the methodology page.

What if my country is not well supported by the brand?

Check for an official importer, a real warranty and local service before you buy, and make sure the model works with your mobile operator and your power supply.
