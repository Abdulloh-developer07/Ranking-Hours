Gaming Hardware Comparisons

Real-world comparisons of consoles, gaming PCs, headsets, mice, keyboards, and VR headsets.

How We Test

A benchmark score alone doesn't tell you much about gaming hardware - real advice needs real hours of testing. We put every component - processors, graphics cards, power supplies, cooling - through multi-day testing sessions, tracking frame consistency, input delay, and noise under maximum load.

We test in temperature-controlled rooms so outside conditions don't skew the results, and our logging tools catch millisecond-level dips that a typical benchmark app would miss entirely. This page covers everything we've learned testing today's gaming hardware, in plain terms.

Raw power alone isn't enough - how well a system manages heat matters just as much. Older hardware would suddenly throttle when it hit thermal limits, causing stutters. Modern systems use onboard sensors to manage power and heat much more smoothly, letting them run at near-peak performance for hours.

PlayStation 5 vs Xbox Series X

4.5 / 5 Core Rating

Sony and Microsoft took genuinely different approaches with these consoles. We put over 100 hours into testing both under heavy workloads, tracking storage speed, performance scaling, and cooling. The PlayStation 5 is built around minimizing loading - its custom SSD controller moves data into memory so fast that developers can skip traditional loading screens almost entirely.

The Xbox Series X leans on raw computing power instead, with more compute units for strong overall performance. Its split memory setup can occasionally cause minor frame hitches if a game doesn't manage it well, something we caught in our detailed frame-time testing. Bottom line: the Series X edges out the PS5 on resolution, but the PS5 delivers slightly more consistent frame pacing thanks to its simpler, unified memory design.

Cooling also differs: Sony uses a large heatsink with liquid metal to pull heat off the chip, while Microsoft uses a vapor chamber and a big fan in a vertical layout that stays quiet even under heavy ray-tracing loads. In the end, it comes down to what you value more - the PS5's fast loading and exclusive games, or the Xbox's raw power and subscription library.

Both controllers have very low input latency. Sony's DualSense uses Bluetooth tuned to keep delay in the single-digit milliseconds even while driving its haptics and adaptive triggers. Microsoft's proprietary wireless protocol holds a rock-solid connection over longer distances. In our multi-day testing, both held up reliably even in busy wireless environments.

Building a Gaming PC

4.0 / 5 Core Rating

Building your own PC means understanding how parts interact, so you don't create a bottleneck. A common mistake is pairing a powerful CPU with a cheap motherboard - its weaker power delivery can overheat under sustained load, forcing the system to throttle and wasting the money spent on the CPU.

For mid-range builds, it's about value per dollar. Mid-tier graphics cards perform well but rely on features like frame generation and upscaling to hit high frame rates, and that needs fast, well-built memory to avoid stutter. In our testing, a better motherboard and fast memory made a bigger real-world difference than buying the most expensive graphics card on a weak platform.

For high-end builds, power delivery and cooling matter most. These systems draw serious power under load, so a quality power supply with stable components prevents random shutdowns during demanding scenes. Cooling usually means moving beyond air coolers to a custom liquid loop, which takes some care to set up properly but keeps components running at peak speed long-term.

Storage has shifted almost entirely to fast SSDs, which need their own heatsinks - heavy workloads can otherwise overheat the drive's controller and slow it down. Every part of the build matters, from the power supply down to the thermal paste under the cooler.

Gaming Headsets

5.0 / 5 Core Rating

Gaming headsets need to balance acoustic design, driver materials, and signal processing to deliver an accurate soundstage - in competitive games, sound is a real gameplay tool for locating enemies. Higher-end headsets use rigid materials like beryllium or graphene in their drivers, which keeps bass from muddying details like footsteps.

We compared SteelSeries, Razer, and Logitech using dummy-head microphones to measure real acoustic accuracy - and physical design matters far more than marketing terms suggest. Closed-back headsets isolate outside noise well and deliver strong bass, but can create internal echo without good sound dampening. Open-back headsets sound more natural and spacious, at the cost of blocking zero outside noise.

Wireless headsets now use dedicated low-latency dongles instead of standard Bluetooth, keeping audio quality high without draining the battery. Microphones have improved too, with noise-canceling that filters out background sound so your voice comes through clearly even in a noisy room.

Battery placement matters for comfort - putting it all in one earcup creates uneven weight that strains your neck over long sessions. Better headsets split the weight evenly or use a headband designed to distribute it, paired with breathable ear cushions.

Gaming Mice

3.5 / 5 Core Rating

Gaming mice have come a long way from a basic desk accessory. The optical sensor takes thousands of tiny photos of the surface every second to track movement precisely, and modern sensors auto-calibrate their tracking height, so the cursor doesn't drift when you lift and reposition the mouse.

We tested sensor reliability across different speeds for weeks. Polling rate matters a lot: older mice update 1,000 times a second, while modern competitive mice go up to 8,000 times a second, cutting input latency dramatically. That said, it does demand more from your PC's processor, and a weak system can actually see odd frame drops from all that constant input data.

Weight and shell design affect precision too. Premium mice now weigh under 60 grams even without the honeycomb-style holes older lightweight mice needed, thanks to smarter internal reinforcement. Button switches have also shifted from traditional mechanical contacts to optical switches, which use a light beam instead of bending metal - this eliminates accidental double-clicks and lasts far longer.

Wireless mice now match or beat wired connections for speed and reliability, constantly scanning for a clear channel to avoid interference. Even the mouse feet have improved - smoother, more durable materials glide better across mousepads, which helps build consistent muscle memory over time.

Gaming Keyboards

4.0 / 5 Core Rating

Gaming keyboards have moved well beyond basic plastic switches. Traditional mechanical switches use metal contacts that meet to register a keypress: linear switches feel smooth and are favored for fast gaming inputs, tactile switches add a bump you can feel at the registration point, and clicky switches add sound to that bump. Each type suits different typing preferences.

We tested actuation force and input delay across major brands, and the big trend is adjustable magnetic switches. Instead of fixed metal contacts, magnetic sensors measure exactly how far a key is pressed, letting you set the actuation point for each key individually through software - shallow for speed on key actions, deeper to avoid accidental presses elsewhere.

Build quality matters too - budget keyboards use thin plastic that flexes under heavy typing, while higher-end ones use solid aluminum plates with sound dampening. Better keycaps also resist wearing shiny from finger oils over time, and detachable cables add durability.

Modern keyboards also use faster internal chips that track every key at once, so nothing gets dropped during frantic multi-key inputs, and RGB lighting runs on its own power line so it doesn't add any input delay.

VR Headsets

3.5 / 5 Core Rating

VR headsets are one of the toughest engineering challenges out there - the display, lenses, and head tracking all have to work in near-perfect sync. Any noticeable lag between moving your head and the image updating causes motion sickness, so headsets need extremely high refresh rates and very low latency, along with dense displays that hide the gaps between pixels.

We tested standalone headsets against PC-tethered ones, and there's a clear trade-off. Standalone headsets pack the chip, battery, and cooling into the headset itself, giving you freedom of movement, but have to scale back visual quality to avoid overheating. PC-tethered headsets stream full-quality video from a powerful graphics card, giving you better visuals at the cost of being tied to a cable.

Lens design has improved a lot too - older headsets had a small sweet spot with blurry edges, while modern pancake lenses fold the light path to make the headset thinner and lighter on your face. Tracking has also moved from needing external wall sensors to cameras built right into the headset, which map your room and controllers automatically.

Some premium headsets also add eye tracking, which focuses rendering power only on exactly where you're looking and blurs the rest - this mimics natural vision and saves a lot of processing power. Combined with better head straps and small fans that prevent lens fog, VR hardware keeps getting closer to feeling truly immersive.
