How Home Audio Has Changed

Home theater setups have shifted away from bulky multi-speaker systems toward sleek, all-in-one soundbars. As TVs get thinner, there's less room for built-in speakers to produce real bass, which is why even expensive TVs often sound thin and tinny on their own. Modern soundbars fix this by packing multiple amplifiers and speakers into one compact unit. Brands like Samsung, LG, and Sonos don't just make dialogue clearer anymore - they recreate a genuine theater-like sound in your living room.

Creating a wide, immersive soundstage without cluttering your room with speaker wires takes real engineering, and each brand takes a different approach. Samsung focuses on more physical speakers and tight integration with its own TVs. LG focuses on smart, automatic sound processing. Sonos focuses on clean streaming and the ability to expand your system over time. Here's how each one actually performs.

Samsung: Raw Power and TV Integration

Samsung's soundbars are built around one goal: pack in as many real speakers as possible. Their top models include physical surround speakers and a dedicated subwoofer right in the box. Instead of relying only on software tricks, Samsung places speakers at specific angles so sound genuinely bounces off your walls and ceiling, giving movies a real sense of movement.

If you own a matching Samsung TV, there's a nice bonus - the TV's built-in speakers can work together with the soundbar, making dialogue sound like it's coming directly from the actors on screen. That link keeps high-frequency detail sharp, while the wireless subwoofer handles deep bass easily.

The tradeoff is size - Samsung's soundbars tend to be long and bulky, which can look out of place under a slim TV stand. You get a lot of manual control to fine-tune each channel, but the automatic room calibration isn't as polished as some competitors. Samsung is a great fit if you want maximum volume and speaker count and don't mind running a few extra wires.

LG: Smart Processing and a Slim Design

LG leans into smart software and a sleek design that fits neatly under a thin TV. LG partners with well-known audio engineers to tune their speakers for accurate, studio-like sound.

The standout feature is LG's real-time audio adjustment - it analyzes what you're watching scene by scene and adjusts automatically: sharper highs during quiet dialogue, a wider sound field during sports, deeper bass during action scenes. It also uses built-in microphones to scan your room's shape and furniture, adjusting for your specific space in seconds.

In practice, this gives you clear dialogue and a wide front soundstage. The tradeoff is that rear-surround effects are mostly created digitally rather than with real rear speakers, so fast sounds behind you can feel a bit less precise than with a system that has dedicated rear boxes. LG is a great pick if you want something smart and plug-and-play that looks great and just works.

Sonos: Clean Streaming and Room to Grow

Sonos takes a different approach entirely, focusing on clean software, premium build quality, and the ability to expand your system over time. Rather than selling one big bundle, Sonos sells a single bar that sounds great on its own, letting you add a subwoofer or rear speakers later as your budget allows. The build quality feels genuinely premium, with smooth finishes that blend into modern living rooms.

The real advantage of Sonos is how it streams audio - instead of connecting over your phone's Bluetooth (which compresses the sound), it connects directly to your home network through the Sonos app, streaming full-quality audio straight from music services. That makes it just as good for everyday music listening as it is for watching TV.

Sonos calibrates itself using your phone's microphone - you walk around your room while it plays test tones, and it tunes the sound to your space automatically. The main downside is connectivity - since Sonos is built around wireless, most bars only have a single physical port, so your TV needs to handle switching between other devices like consoles. Sonos is the best pick if you want clean, simple control and the option to grow into a full home sound system later.

How Your Room Actually Affects Sound

It's easy to forget that your actual living room shapes how a soundbar performs. Sound bounces off hard surfaces like glass and hardwood floors, and gets absorbed by soft things like rugs and curtains. A soundbar that sounds amazing in a carpeted showroom can sound harsh or echoey in an apartment with bare floors and high ceilings.

This is where the differences between brands really show. Systems that bounce sound off the ceiling need a flat, hard ceiling to work well - if you have exposed beams or vaulted ceilings, that effect can get muddled. In tricky rooms like that, Samsung's manual adjustments or Sonos's room scanning become genuinely useful tools for balancing things out.

Audio Connections and Streaming Quality

For serious viewers, how a soundbar receives audio matters just as much as its speaker count. High-quality surround formats need a lot of data, more than a basic optical cable can carry - which is why modern setups use a high-speed connection (eARC) between your TV and soundbar to keep everything perfectly in sync.

For music, wireless streaming from your phone often compresses the audio and loses some detail. Sonos avoids this by pulling music directly from the internet over your home network instead. Samsung and LG also support higher-quality wireless streaming on compatible phones, so you can still get solid sound quality wirelessly.

Long-Term Value and Expanding Your System

A good soundbar is a long-term purchase, so it's worth thinking about whether you can add to it later. A lot of people start with just a single bar and later want to add surround speakers as their space or budget grows.

Samsung gives you a lot of hardware upfront, but it's typically sold as a fixed, complete kit - you can't easily swap the subwoofer for a bigger one or add extra channels later. Sonos is the opposite - fully modular. You can start with just the bar, add a subwoofer a year later, then add rear speakers whenever you're ready. That flexibility protects your investment as your setup grows.
