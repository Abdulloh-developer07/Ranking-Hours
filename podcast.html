Podcast & streaming gear

Build a setup that sounds good before it looks impressive

Most people buy the microphone first and the room last, which is exactly backwards. This guide walks through the chain in the order that actually improves your sound, then gives three realistic budget builds.

Chapter 01

The room comes first

A podcast microphone does not only record your voice. It records the room your voice is in. Hard, empty surfaces such as bare walls, a glass window, a tiled floor and a bare desk bounce sound back at the microphone a few milliseconds late. Your ear ignores this in person, but a recording keeps it, and the result is that hollow, echoey sound people describe as "sounding like a bathroom".

The fix is not expensive. Soft, uneven surfaces absorb and scatter that reflected sound: curtains, a rug, a bookshelf full of books, a sofa, a bed with a duvet on it. Recording in a small room full of clothes, such as a walk-in closet, works far better than most purpose-built "studio" corners. If you can only change one thing, put something soft behind the microphone and something soft on the wall you are facing while you speak.

Noise you have stopped hearing

Your brain filters out steady background sounds, and a microphone does not. Before you record, stand in the room and listen for a full minute. A refrigerator compressor, a computer fan, an air conditioner, a fluorescent light, a neighbour's television and traffic through a thin window all show up in a recording. Turn off what you can, move the computer or use a longer cable, and choose the quietest hours of the day. A quiet room and a cheap microphone beat a noisy room and an expensive one nearly every time.

Small changes that cost almost nothing

You can improve most rooms in an afternoon without buying anything. Record with your back to a wall of soft furnishings rather than facing a window. Hang a thick blanket or duvet behind the microphone position, or on a clothes rack you can move. Put a rug or folded towels on a bare desk, because a hard tabletop reflects your voice straight up into the microphone. Close the door, switch off fans and put your phone in another room or in airplane mode, since phones can cause buzzing when they search for a signal near a microphone cable.

Later, if you want something more permanent, acoustic foam and panels help, but treat them as an extra rather than a starting point. Thin foam mostly reduces high-frequency echo and does little for low rumble, so a thick blanket often does more per pound spent than a pack of small foam tiles.

A quick room test

Clap once in the middle of the room. If you hear a short metallic ring or a fluttering echo after the clap, the room is reflective. Try again after adding a blanket or a couple of pillows around you. You will hear the difference immediately, and recording a short test with your phone before you buy anything tells you more about your setup than any spec sheet.

Chapter 02

Microphones: dynamic or condenser, USB or XLR

Two choices decide most of what you need to know. The first is how the microphone works, and the second is how it connects to your computer.

Dynamic versus condenser

Dynamic microphones are tougher and less sensitive. They pick up what is close to them and mostly ignore what is far away, which makes them forgiving in an untreated room. Classic broadcast microphones are dynamic. Well-known examples include the Shure SM58 and SM7B, the Rode PodMic and the Samson Q2U.

Condenser microphones are more sensitive and pick up more detail, including the detail you did not want: fan noise, keyboard clicks and room echo. They can sound wonderful in a quiet, treated room. They usually need phantom power, a 48-volt supply from an interface, and they are more fragile. A large-diameter condenser in an untreated bedroom is one of the most common bad purchases in home recording.

For a spoken-word podcast in a normal home, a dynamic microphone is usually the safer starting point. Condensers make more sense when you have a quiet, soft room and want extra clarity for voice-over, singing or an instrument.

USB versus XLR

A USB microphone has a small built-in converter and plugs straight into a computer. It is the simplest route: one cable, no extra box. The downside is that you cannot upgrade the pieces separately, and using two USB microphones on one computer can be awkward with some software.

An XLR microphone uses a three-pin professional connector and needs an interface or mixer between it and your computer. It costs more and takes longer to set up, but you can swap the microphone, the preamp or the interface on their own, and you can add a second or third microphone later. Many popular models, such as the Samson Q2U and Audio-Technica ATR2100x-USB, offer both connections, so you can start on USB and move to XLR later without buying again.

Gain-hungry microphones: Some famous dynamic microphones, the SM7B being the best-known, need a lot of clean amplification. A cheap interface may sound thin or hissy at high gain, and people often add an inline booster. Check how much gain a microphone needs before you pair it with an interface.

More than one microphone in a room

With two people in the same room, every microphone also hears the other person. That bleed makes editing harder, because you cannot cut one voice without hearing the other underneath it. Sit as far apart as the room allows, point each microphone away from the other speaker, and keep each person close to their own microphone. Dynamic microphones are again the easier choice here, because they pick up less from the sides. If you can, record each person on a separate track so you can fix one voice without touching the other.

Technique matters more than price

Keep the microphone about a hand's width from your mouth, roughly a fist and a thumb, and speak slightly off-axis so hard consonants like "p" and "b" do not burst into the capsule. A pop filter or foam cover helps with that. A boom arm lifts the microphone off the desk, which keeps typing and table bumps out of the recording and lets you place the microphone at a steady distance. Keep that distance the same throughout the recording, because moving closer and further away is the fastest way to make audio hard to edit.

Chapter 03

Interfaces, mixers and all-in-one consoles

An audio interface turns the analogue signal from an XLR microphone into digital audio your computer can record, and it gives you a gain knob and a headphone output. A basic two-input interface is enough for one or two people. Look for a clean preamp, a headphone output with real volume, a direct monitoring switch that lets you hear yourself without delay, and a driver that works with your operating system.

A small mixer or an all-in-one podcast console adds more: multiple headphone outputs, sound pads for jingles, phone or Bluetooth inputs and separate faders per person. These are more comfortable when you have a co-host or regular guests in the same room. The trade-off is size, price and a longer learning curve. If you record alone, a two-input interface is almost always enough.

Portable recorders are a different tool. They record to a memory card without a computer, which suits interviews outside the studio. Many also work as a USB interface, so one device can serve two purposes.

Chapter 04

Headphones and monitoring

You cannot fix what you cannot hear. Closed-back headphones seal around the ear and keep the sound from leaking into your microphone, which is why they are the standard for recording. Open-back headphones sound more natural for editing but leak sound, so they are better for listening after you have finished recording.

Comfort matters more than most reviews admit, because you may wear them for two hours at a time. Studio models such as the Audio-Technica ATH-M50x and Sony MDR-7506 are common recommendations because they are plain, sturdy and reveal problems rather than flattering the sound. Bluetooth headphones are a poor choice for recording because of the delay between what you say and what you hear.

Always listen back on something else before you publish. Play the recording on a phone speaker, a car stereo and cheap earbuds. Most of your audience will listen that way, and a voice that sounds thin or muddy in earbuds will not be fixed by a beautiful mix in studio headphones.

Chapter 05

Software and recording settings

You do not need to pay for software to start. Audacity is free and handles recording and editing on most systems. GarageBand is free on Apple devices. Reaper is a paid editor with a generous evaluation period and a low licence price, and text-based editors let you cut audio by deleting words in a transcript, which some people find much faster.

Settings that avoid problems later

  • Record at 48 kHz and 24-bit if your software allows it. 44.1 kHz is also fine, but pick one and stay consistent across projects.
  • Save a WAV file as your master, and export MP3 or AAC for publishing. A speech-only show does not need a huge file: a mono export at a moderate bitrate keeps downloads light.
  • Set your gain so normal speech peaks around minus 12 to minus 6 decibels on the meter. Leave headroom for laughter or a sudden shout, because clipped audio cannot be repaired.
  • Aim to publish at roughly minus 16 LUFS for stereo or minus 19 LUFS for mono, which is the loudness most podcast platforms and listeners are used to.

A light editing pass

Good editing is mostly subtraction. Cut long silences, false starts and obvious mistakes, then stop. Over-processed speech sounds robotic, and the more you fix in software, the more you hear the fix. A simple order works for most voices.

  • High-pass filter. Roll off everything below roughly 80 Hz. Speech does not need it, and it removes desk rumble and air-conditioning hum.
  • Gentle noise reduction. Use it only if there is a steady hiss, and use the smallest setting that helps. Heavy settings make voices sound watery.
  • Light compression. A mild compressor evens out the difference between quiet and loud words so listeners do not keep reaching for the volume knob.
  • Small equalizer moves. A slight cut around the muddy low-mid range, and a slight lift for clarity, is often all a voice needs.
  • Loudness last. Set the final level after everything else, then listen to the whole episode once at normal volume before you export.

Do not try to remove every breath and every "um". A recording with every natural pause removed sounds tiring. Take out the ones that distract, and leave the rest.

Recording with remote guests

A video call on its own compresses audio and can sound uneven when someone's connection drops. Remote-recording tools solve this by recording each person locally on their own device and uploading the file afterwards. Ask every guest to wear headphones, because speakers cause echo, and to sit near a soft surface. If your guest has no microphone, a phone headset usually sounds better than a laptop's built-in one.

Chapter 06

Adding video for streaming

Once audio is solid, streaming or recording video is a separate set of choices. Viewers forgive an average picture but not bad sound, which is why audio comes first in this guide. When you are ready, three parts matter.

Camera

A good webcam is enough to begin. It is small and simple, and modern models handle 1080p well in decent light. A mirrorless or compact camera with a clean HDMI output looks sharper with a softer background, but it needs a capture device, a power solution and more setup. Some cameras overheat or time out during long sessions, so check that a model supports continuous use before buying.

Lighting

Light makes a bigger visible difference than a more expensive camera. Put a soft light source in front of you, slightly above eye level and to one side. A window works well during the day if it is in front of you and not behind you. Avoid a single bright lamp behind you, because the camera will expose for the light and turn your face into a silhouette.

Game audio, chat and your voice

If you stream games, you are mixing several sound sources: the game, your voice, music and perhaps voice chat with friends. Send each one to its own track in OBS so you can adjust them separately. Add a noise gate to your microphone so keyboard clicks are muted between sentences, and a light compressor so sudden shouts do not overwhelm the mix. Keep game audio a little lower than you think it should be, because your voice is what viewers came to hear. Wear headphones the whole time. If game sound leaks from speakers into your microphone, viewers hear an echo of the game and a delayed version of themselves.

Bandwidth and software

OBS Studio is free and handles scenes, overlays and multiple sources. For live streaming, upload speed matters more than download speed. A 1080p stream commonly uses around 6 megabits per second, and you should have comfortably more than that available, ideally over a wired connection. Wi-Fi works, but a cable is more stable when a stream lasts for hours. Run a short private test stream first to check both sound and picture.

Chapter 07

Three realistic builds

Prices change and vary by country, so treat these as a rough shape rather than a shopping list. Check current offers before you buy.

Starter

Simple and quiet

  • USB dynamic microphone that also has an XLR output
  • Foam pop filter and a desk stand or basic arm
  • Wired closed-back headphones
  • Free software, blankets and pillows for the room
Growing

Room to expand

  • XLR dynamic microphone and boom arm
  • Two-input interface with a clean preamp
  • Studio closed-back headphones
  • Portable sound panels behind and beside you
Serious

Two people, video too

  • Two matched XLR microphones on arms
  • Podcast console or a four-input interface
  • Two pairs of headphones, plus a splitter
  • Camera with a capture device and soft lighting

Notice what is missing from each list: nothing has to be expensive in the first tier. The jump from the first tier to the second buys you flexibility more than a dramatic improvement in sound. The jump to the third tier only makes sense if you have a regular co-host, an audience that expects video, or a client who pays you for the result.

Chapter 08

Mistakes that waste the most money

  • Buying a sensitive condenser for a noisy room. It will faithfully record every fan and passing car. Fix the room, or choose a dynamic microphone.
  • Skipping headphones. Without monitoring, you find out about hum, clipping and echo after the recording is over.
  • Buying gear for a show you have not started. Record a few episodes on something simple, and then you will know which limit actually bothers you.
  • Ignoring cables and stands. A cheap cable that hums or a wobbly desk stand that transmits every tap can spoil a good microphone.
  • Chasing perfect audio instead of publishing. Listeners forgive a slightly imperfect sound. They do not forgive a show that never comes out.
  • Not making a backup. Record to more than one place when the conversation matters, and copy the files to a second drive the same day.
Chapter 09

Checklist and questions

  • Is the room soft enough that a clap does not ring?
  • Does the microphone suit the room: dynamic for normal rooms, condenser only for quiet, treated ones?
  • Can you monitor what you record through wired closed-back headphones?
  • Do the connectors match: XLR or USB, and does the interface supply what the microphone needs?
  • Have you tested a full recording and listened on a phone before deciding to upgrade?

Common questions

Do I need an interface if I use a USB microphone? No. A USB microphone has one built in. You would need an interface only if you switch to an XLR microphone.

Is a more expensive microphone always better? No. It may be more detailed, but detail is not always what a bad room needs. Price says little about how a microphone will sound in your space.

How long should I use one setup before upgrading? Long enough to know what specific problem you are trying to solve. If you can name it, such as too much room echo or not enough inputs, you can shop for it. If you cannot, wait.

Can I record a good podcast on my phone? Yes, in a quiet, soft room with the phone close to your mouth. It is a legitimate way to start, and the habit of publishing matters more than the equipment.
