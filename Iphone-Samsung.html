iPhone vs Samsung phones: the everyday differences

Forget the spec sheets for a moment. This is about what changes in your day when you carry an iPhone, and what changes when you carry a Galaxy.

  • you own a Mac, an Apple Watch or AirPods

A Samsung Galaxy suits you if

  • you want to choose from many sizes and prices
  • you like to change how your phone looks and works

Two very different ways to sell phones

Apple makes a small number of iPhones each year and keeps them in a tidy line. There is a standard model, a larger one, two Pro models, and in recent years a cheaper model for people on a budget. Every one of them runs the same software, looks familiar, and is handled the same way by the shop.

Samsung does the opposite. It sells phones at nearly every price, from simple models for a few hundred dollars to expensive flagships, plus foldables that open like a book or snap shut like a compact mirror. The Galaxy A series covers the budget and middle, the Galaxy S series covers the flagships, and the Z series covers the folding phones. The top S model, the Ultra, even has a pen that slides into the body.

This difference explains most of what follows. With an iPhone you pick a size and a storage amount, and you are done. With a Galaxy you have more choices, which is a gift if you know what you want and a headache if you do not. Neither approach is better. It depends on whether you like being guided or you like options.

This page looks at ordinary life with each phone: setting it up, messaging, taking pictures, adding apps, charging, repairing and selling. We built it from the manufacturers' published information and from what owners and reviewers commonly report. We have not measured these phones ourselves, so you will not find lab numbers here. For a model-by-model look at two specific phones, see our Pixel 8 and iPhone 15 comparison.

Comparing like with like

A common mistake is to put a cheap Samsung next to an expensive iPhone, or the other way around, and then declare a winner. The result says nothing. Compare phones that cost about the same.

  • Budget: a Galaxy A series phone against Apple's cheapest current iPhone. Samsung usually gives you a bigger screen and more storage for the money. Apple gives you a faster chip and longer software life.
  • Mid-range flagship: a standard Galaxy S against a standard iPhone. This is the most even fight, and it is where the points below apply most directly.
  • Top end: a Galaxy S Ultra against an iPhone Pro Max. Both are packed with extras. Samsung leads on zoom and has the pen, while Apple leads on video and chip speed.
  • Folding: Samsung has this field mostly to itself. If a folding phone interests you, the choice is not really between two brands.

The first week

Setting up a new phone is a good test of how a company thinks. On an iPhone you hold the old one near the new one, follow a few prompts, and most of your apps, settings and photos come across. Moving from another iPhone is smoother than from anything else. If you come from Android, Apple provides an app that helps, and it works for contacts, photos, calendars and messages, though not for paid apps.

Samsung has a similar tool called Smart Switch, and it is good. Moving from an old Samsung phone is easy, and moving from an iPhone works too, either with a cable or over Wi-Fi. It brings over many apps, your contacts, your photos and often your layout. If you have been on Android for years, a Galaxy feels familiar from the first minute.

The surprising bits tend to show up later. People who change brands often report they spend the first week hunting for the things they used to do without thinking: where is the back button, how do I close an app, where did my notifications go. The gestures are different on each system. Give yourself a week before you decide you dislike a phone. Most of the early irritation is habit and not a flaw in the phone.

Before you switch, do a short preparation. Back up your photos, write down the passwords you do not remember, and check that your two-factor codes do not live inside an app that will be left behind. Messaging apps need care, so read the instructions for moving chat history from the app maker. If you use a bank app that is tied to your old device, find out how to transfer it before you wipe the old phone.

Messages and calls

This is the area where iPhone owners feel the strongest pull. iMessage is built in, quick, and used by nearly everyone in some friend groups and families. Texts between iPhones look different, arrive with extras like reactions and high-quality photos, and are encrypted. If everyone you talk to has an iPhone, an Android phone makes those chats feel a bit second-class.

The gap has narrowed. Apple now supports RCS, the newer text standard that Android phones have used for some time, so messages between an iPhone and a Galaxy are better than the old plain SMS. Photos are sharper, typing indicators work, and group chats behave better. Some iMessage features still stay inside Apple's world, and cross-platform chats are not always as private as iMessage between two iPhones, so do not expect a perfect match.

For most people the real question is which apps their friends use. If your circle talks on WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram or Signal, the phone brand hardly matters, because those apps look and work the same on both. If your circle lives in iMessage and FaceTime, an iPhone saves you friction. In many countries these apps dominate and iMessage is hardly used, so the pull is weaker.

On calls, both phones are fine. Samsung phones offer built-in call recording and a feature that translates a call as you talk, in the languages and countries where it is available. Apple has added call screening and some call features of its own. Rules about call recording differ from country to country, so check the law where you live before you use it.

Photos and video

Both brands take excellent pictures, and most people could not tell which phone took a photo in good light if you removed the names. The differences are in style and in the extras.

Samsung tends to produce bright, colourful, punchy photos with a lot of sharpening, which looks great on social media. Apple's look is more natural and softer, with a warm tone and restrained contrast. Many people like one and dislike the other, and no argument will change that. The best way to decide is to look at photos from owners of both phones, taken in conditions like yours, and see which look you prefer.

Zoom is where Samsung shows off. The S Ultra models carry a long zoom lens and a very high-resolution main sensor, and they are better than most phones at photographing things far away: a stage at a concert, a bird, a sign across a street. Apple's Pro models also have a zoom lens, though the standard iPhone relies on cropping into its sensor. If you often shoot distant subjects, a Samsung Ultra is hard to beat.

Video is the iPhone's strength. The footage is stable, the colours stay consistent when you walk from a bright room to a dark one, and the sound is good. Many people who make videos for a living use iPhones for this reason. Samsung records excellent video too, and its latest models are close, but reviewers still often give the edge to Apple, especially for video calls and clips shot while walking.

On editing, Samsung gives you a rich set of tools in its gallery, such as object removal and AI-assisted edits, while iPhone has its own clean-up and editing features in Photos. Both can crop, adjust, remove blemishes and sort pictures by person and place. The tools are good enough that most people will never need a separate app.

A small but practical point: Samsung's camera app has many modes and settings, which suits people who like control. Apple's is simpler and nearly impossible to confuse, which suits people who just want to point and shoot.

Home screen, settings and extra apps

Android gives you control of nearly everything on the screen. You can place icons wherever you like, switch to a different launcher, add widgets that show real information, pick another keyboard, set default apps for almost any task and change the look with themes. Samsung adds its own tools on top, such as a Good Lock set of tweaks and Edge panels that slide in from the side of the screen. If you like to set things up your way, you will have fun.

iPhones are more restrained. Apple has added widgets, a customisable lock screen, and the ability to colour or hide icons, so a modern iPhone is more personal than it used to be. But the home screen has rules, and the system decides how a lot of things look. For many people that is a feature, since the phone remains tidy and familiar.

Samsung phones come with some apps you may not want. Alongside Google's apps, there are Samsung's own versions of a browser, a calendar, a gallery and a few more, and some carriers add their own. They can be disabled, but they do take a moment to tidy up. Apple also includes apps you may never use, although you can remove most of them. Neither is perfect, so spend ten minutes on a new phone hiding what you do not need.

Installing apps also differs. Android lets you install apps from sources other than the official store if you choose to. That is useful for some tools and risky if you are careless. Apple has traditionally allowed only its own store, with some changes in certain regions. If you value the extra safety of a closed store, the iPhone approach is calmer. If you value freedom, Android is better.

Apps and games

For almost every popular app, you will find a version on both systems. Social media, shopping, banking, streaming, maps, taxis, food delivery, and most games are all there. In rare cases a new app or feature arrives on iPhone first, since many developers test on Apple devices before others. In other cases, an app comes to Android first or has more features there. Check the two or three apps you cannot live without, and you are probably fine.

For mobile gaming, iPhones are more consistent because the hardware changes less and developers can fine-tune games for it. Samsung's top phones are powerful and handle big games very well, and they often have more cooling and gaming settings. Cheaper Galaxy A phones will struggle with the heaviest titles. For casual games, there is no practical difference.

One thing to watch is region. Some apps, especially payment, banking and government apps, depend on the country you live in and on what your bank supports. Always confirm that the services you rely on work on the phone you want. This matters more than any camera spec.

AI features

Both companies now put AI tools in their phones, and both of them were still adding features at the time of writing. Samsung's Galaxy AI includes live translation, text summaries and rewriting tools, photo editing, and the ability to circle something on the screen to search for it. Apple's tools cover writing help, notification summaries, photo clean-up and a smarter assistant, and they work on newer iPhones only.

Three practical things to know. First, these tools only run on certain models, so a cheaper or older phone from either brand may not have them. Second, features can depend on your language and country, and some are slow to arrive in smaller markets. Third, a few of the features need an internet connection and may become paid later, so read the fine print on what is free.

Our advice is to treat AI as a nice extra and not the reason to buy. The basics, such as a good screen, a good battery and a reliable camera, matter more for most people. If you do want the tools, check that your exact model, language and country are supported before you pay.

Battery, charging and accessories

Battery life depends on the model and on how you use the phone. In general, Samsung's larger phones have bigger batteries and iPhones have efficient chips that stretch a smaller battery a long way. For ordinary use, both manage a day, and heavy users charge in the evening on either.

Charging speed is not either brand's strong point. Both are slower than some Chinese Android makers, whose phones can fill up in well under an hour. Samsung's top models charge a little faster than the standard iPhone, but not by a lot. Neither brand includes a charger in the box in many countries, so plan to buy one. Both now use USB-C, so the same cable and charger can power your phone, tablet and laptop.

Wireless charging works on both. Apple's MagSafe magnets make it easy to attach a charger, a wallet or a car mount, and an ecosystem of accessories has grown around it. Samsung phones support wireless charging too, though magnetic accessories need a special case on most models. If you love accessories that snap on, the iPhone is the more natural fit.

Cases and screen protectors are easy to find for both brands, though iPhone gets a bigger choice in most shops. For Samsung's Ultra phones, remember that a case should leave room for the pen.

Keeping a phone for years

This is where the numbers add up. A phone you keep for five years costs half as much per year as one you replace every two and a half, and it also produces less waste.

Samsung has promised up to seven years of software updates on its recent flagships, and several years on its mid-range models. That is a big promise, and it matches or beats what Apple has said in public. Apple makes no formal promise, but iPhones have a long record of receiving new versions for around five to seven years.

Battery wear is the part that usually decides when a phone is finished. After two or three years, a battery holds less charge than it did at the start. Both brands will replace the battery for a fee, and doing it can add years to a phone's life. Apple's service network is large in most places. Samsung's is large too, and in many countries Samsung has official service centres. Ask about the price of a battery replacement before you buy.

Repairs are a mixed picture. Both have improved their repair programs in recent years, and parts are easier to get than they used to be. Screens crack on both, so a case and a screen protector are cheap insurance.

Privacy and security

Apple has built part of its brand on privacy. It controls the software and the store, and it gives you clear prompts when an app asks for your location, camera or contacts. Samsung uses Android's privacy controls, adds its own security chip and a feature called Knox, and also shows a privacy dashboard that lets you see which apps used which permissions.

In practice, both phones are secure if you keep them updated, use a strong lock and avoid installing strange apps. Most problems for ordinary people come from phishing messages, fake apps and reused passwords, not from a flaw in the phone. A password manager, two-factor login and a habit of ignoring suspicious links do more for you than the choice of brand.

One difference worth knowing: because Android lets you install apps from outside the store, you have a little more responsibility. If you stick to the official store, the risk is small.

Watches, earbuds and trackers

The phone you buy decides which accessories work best. An Apple Watch needs an iPhone, and a Galaxy Watch is made to work with Android and gets the most from a Samsung phone. AirPods work on Android through Bluetooth, but you lose features like automatic switching and some controls. Galaxy Buds work on iPhones too, but with fewer features than on a Samsung phone.

For tracking lost items, Apple's Find My network is large and works with AirTags, and it is very good at finding a lost bag or set of keys in a city. Samsung's SmartTag works inside Samsung's network, which is smaller. If you misplace things often, this is worth thinking about.

For a smart home, Samsung's SmartThings app connects many appliances, TVs and lights, especially other Samsung products. Apple's Home app works with many accessories too. If you own a Samsung TV, fridge or washing machine, the Galaxy phone adds some convenient controls. If you have Apple TV, HomePod or a Mac, the iPhone brings the same kind of extra convenience.

The real cost over time

Price on the shelf is only the first number. A better way to compare phones is to ask what you will spend per year of use, after you subtract what you can sell the old phone for.

iPhones keep their value well. A used iPhone sells for a large share of its original price, even after two or three years, so the total cost of owning one can be lower than the sticker price suggests, if you sell when it is still in good condition. Samsung phones lose value faster, which hurts when you sell and helps when you buy used, because last year's Galaxy can be a bargain. Samsung also runs frequent discounts, and a flagship's price often falls a lot within months of launch.

If you buy a new phone and keep it until it stops being useful, the Samsung usually costs less per year, since it starts cheaper or gets discounted. If you upgrade often and sell the old one, the iPhone usually costs less. Add in a case, a charger and a screen protector, and compare the total.

Also think about trade-in. Both companies and many shops will give you credit for an old phone. The offer depends on the condition and the model, and it is usually lower than what you would get selling privately, but it is simple and quick.

Buying where support is limited

Not every country has the same level of service for both brands. Samsung has a wide network and official stock in many markets. Apple has a strong presence in some countries and a thinner one in others, with phones often arriving through resellers.

Before you buy, ask who offers the warranty and where repairs are done. A local official warranty is worth more than a lower price from an unofficial seller. Ask whether the phone was made for your region, since models sold in different countries can use different network bands. In particular, iPhones sold in the United States have no physical SIM tray and use an eSIM only, and not every mobile operator supports eSIM. If you buy an imported phone, check that it works with your operator before you leave the shop.

Check the box contents, the charger type and the phone's language support too. Some imported phones lack local language keyboards or features that depend on region, such as call recording or certain AI tools.

Who should pick which

Pick a Samsung Galaxy if

  • you want a wide choice of sizes and prices;
  • you like to change how the phone looks and works;
  • you want a pen, a folding screen or a long zoom lens;
  • you already use a Samsung TV, watch, earbuds or a Windows computer;
  • you plan to keep the phone for many years and want a written update promise;
  • you want a good deal and are happy to buy last year's model.

Pick an iPhone if

  • you want a simple phone that behaves the same way every time;
  • you own a Mac, an iPad, an Apple Watch or AirPods;
  • your family and friends use iMessage and FaceTime;
  • video quality matters to you;
  • you want the best resale value;
  • you like the large range of cases and magnetic accessories.

If you are still stuck, ask yourself one question: do I want a phone that is easy, or a phone that is mine? Most people who answer "easy" end up happy with an iPhone, and most who answer "mine" end up happy with a Galaxy.

Questions people ask

Is Samsung or iPhone better?

Neither wins in every area. iPhones are simpler, keep their value and shoot very good video. Samsung phones offer more choice, more customising and a stronger zoom camera.

Which phone lasts longer?

Both can last five years or more. Samsung gives a written promise of up to seven years on recent flagships, and iPhones have a long history of support.

Is it hard to switch from iPhone to Samsung?

No. Samsung's Smart Switch moves your contacts, photos and many apps. Expect a week of getting used to the new gestures. Check your messaging apps and banking apps before you wipe the old phone.

Is it hard to switch from Samsung to iPhone?

Not very. Apple's transfer app handles contacts, photos and messages. Paid apps must be bought again on the other store.

Which brand has the better camera?

It depends on what you shoot. Samsung is stronger at zoom and vivid colours. iPhone is stronger at video and a natural look.

Does a Samsung phone work with AirPods?

Yes, over Bluetooth. You will lose some Apple-only extras such as quick switching between Apple devices.

Which phone is cheaper in the long run?

If you keep a phone until it wears out, a Samsung usually costs less. If you sell your phone every couple of years, an iPhone often does, because it keeps more of its value.

Are iPhones safer than Samsung phones?

Both are safe when kept up to date. Apple's closed store reduces some risks. Android gives you more freedom, which means a little more care when installing apps.
