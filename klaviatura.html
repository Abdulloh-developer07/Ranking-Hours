Cherry MX Red vs Blue vs Brown: choosing a switch by feel, not by hype

A practical breakdown of the three most common Cherry MX switch families — how they actually feel under your fingers, how loud they are, and which typing or gaming habits each one suits.

Ask ten mechanical keyboard owners which switch is "best" and you'll get ten different answers, and all ten of them will be right for that particular person. Switches are the one component of a keyboard that you interact with thousands of times an hour, so the difference between the right switch and the wrong one shows up almost immediately — in your fingers, in your typing speed, and in whether the person sitting next to you can hear every keystroke.

Cherry MX switches are a useful reference point for this comparison because so many other manufacturers, from budget brands to boutique keyboard makers, design their own switches to feel similar to one of the three classic Cherry families: Red, Blue, and Brown. Understanding what separates them tells you a lot about mechanical switches in general, even if the keyboard you eventually buy uses a different brand's version of the same idea.

The three switch types, in plain terms

All three switches share the same basic travel distance and roughly the same actuation force, which is part of why comparisons that only quote numbers from a spec sheet can be misleading. The real difference is in what happens partway through the keypress — the part your finger actually feels.

MX Red — Linear

A smooth, consistent press from top to bottom with no bump and no click. Nothing interrupts the motion, which is why gamers and fast typists who've built up muscle memory tend to prefer it.

MX Blue — Clicky

A distinct tactile bump paired with an audible click at the actuation point. You feel and hear exactly when a keypress registers, which some typists find satisfying and others find distracting.

MX Brown — Tactile

A tactile bump similar to the Blue's, but without the loud click. A middle-ground switch that tries to give feedback without turning every keystroke into a noise event.

Linear switches like the Red are often the switch of choice for competitive gaming, where a smooth, predictable press supports rapid, repeated key taps without anything physically interrupting the motion. That same smoothness is exactly why some typists find linear switches harder to type accurately on at first — without a bump to signal actuation, it's easier to bottom out every key or to second-guess whether a press registered.

Clicky switches like the Blue give the clearest feedback of the three, both physically and audibly, which a lot of people genuinely enjoy for typing. The trade-off is volume: a room full of Blue switches is genuinely loud, and in a shared office, a video call, or a house with other people trying to sleep, that click can become a real point of friction rather than a charming detail.

Tactile switches like the Brown are frequently recommended as a starting point for someone who has never used a mechanical keyboard before, because they offer a middle ground — enough feedback to feel more deliberate than a laptop keyboard, without the volume of a clicky switch. They're also commonly chosen for keyboards that need to handle both typing and light gaming reasonably well, rather than being optimised heavily toward one or the other.

Sound and shared spaces

Noise deserves more attention in a switch comparison than it usually gets, because it's the factor most likely to create a problem you didn't anticipate. A Blue switch keyboard that sounds satisfying to you during a solo late-night coding session can become a genuine source of tension in a shared apartment, an open office, or on a video call with a microphone that picks up every click. If noise is a real constraint, Brown or Red switches — paired with a case design that doesn't amplify sound, and possibly some foam dampening inside the case — are the more realistic choice, regardless of how appealing the tactile click of a Blue switch sounds in a demo video.

Matching a switch to what you actually do

Case 01

Competitive gaming

MX Red or another linear switch, for the smoothest, most consistent rapid keypresses with nothing physically interrupting the motion.

Case 02

Long writing sessions

MX Brown or MX Blue, depending on tolerance for noise — the tactile feedback helps reduce accidental double presses during long typing stretches.

Case 03

Shared office or open floor plan

MX Red or a dedicated silent-linear switch — anything clicky will draw complaints within the first week.

Case 04

Programming and mixed use

MX Brown is the common middle-ground pick, balancing feedback with a noise level most desks can tolerate.

Case 05

Late-night streaming or calls

MX Red, ideally with a keyboard case that includes foam dampening to further cut down on transmitted key noise.

What matters beyond the switch name

A switch is only one ingredient in how a finished keyboard actually feels, and treating it as the whole story is one of the more common mistakes in a keyboard comparison. Keycap material and shape change the texture under your fingertips and can noticeably alter perceived sound — thicker PBT keycaps generally sound deeper and feel more substantial than thin ABS caps, even with an identical switch underneath. Stabilisers, the small mechanisms under longer keys like the space bar and enter key, are a common source of rattle on budget boards regardless of how good the switches themselves are; lubricated, well-tuned stabilisers make a bigger difference to the overall feel than most people expect going in.

Case and plate material affect both sound and the amount of flex you feel when bottoming out a key — a plastic case tends to sound higher-pitched and hollow, while an aluminium case generally sounds deeper and feels firmer underneath the keys. Layout is a separate decision entirely from switch type: a full-size board keeps a dedicated number pad, a tenkeyless board drops it to save desk space, and increasingly compact 65% and 60% layouts remove navigation clusters and function rows in exchange for a smaller footprint and easier full-desk mouse movement.

Hot-swappable boards

A growing number of keyboards now use hot-swappable switch sockets, meaning individual switches can be pulled out and replaced without any soldering. This turns "which switch should I buy" into a much lower-stakes question, since a hot-swappable board lets you try Red, Blue, and Brown switches in the same keyboard and settle on a favourite through direct comparison rather than a description on a page. For anyone genuinely unsure which switch suits them, a hot-swap board is often a better first purchase than committing to one soldered switch type.

Wired versus wireless

Wired keyboards avoid battery management entirely and generally offer the lowest input latency, which matters most for competitive gaming. Wireless boards add convenience and a cleaner desk, but introduce a charging routine and, on cheaper models, occasionally noticeable input lag — worth checking specifically if the keyboard will be used for anything latency-sensitive.

Where Cherry MX sits among other switch makers

Cherry has been manufacturing these switches since the 1980s, which is why the naming convention — Red for linear, Blue for clicky, Brown for tactile — became a shorthand the entire industry still uses, even for switches that aren't made by Cherry at all. Gateron and Kailh, two of the larger alternative manufacturers, both produce switches sold under the same colour names, generally built to a similar spec but often priced lower and, in the case of many Gateron switches, felt by a lot of enthusiasts as smoother out of the box without any modification.

This matters for a comparison because "Cherry MX Red" and "a red linear switch" are not automatically the same purchase. Budget keyboards frequently use Gateron or Kailh switches described only by colour, and the feel can be close enough that most people wouldn't notice a blind difference, or different enough that switch enthusiasts have strong opinions about which brand's Brown feels more refined. If a specific feel matters enough to you to research switches this closely in the first place, it's worth checking which manufacturer actually made the switches in a given keyboard rather than assuming "Brown" always means the same thing.

Lubing and modifying switches

Enthusiasts commonly apply a thin switch lubricant to reduce scratchiness and spring noise, a process usually called "lubing." It's genuinely effective — a well-lubed linear switch can go from slightly gritty to glass-smooth — but it's also time-consuming, since doing it properly means opening every individual switch, applying lubricant to the internal components, and reassembling the board. For most buyers this isn't a necessary step; factory-lubed switches from reputable manufacturers are perfectly usable out of the box. It becomes relevant mainly for people who've already decided they enjoy the hobby side of mechanical keyboards and want to fine-tune a specific board rather than simply use one.

Keycap material and profile, explained

Keycaps are usually made from one of two plastics. ABS keycaps are typically thinner, produce a slightly higher-pitched sound, and tend to develop a shiny, worn patina on frequently pressed keys after a year or two of use. PBT keycaps are generally thicker, feel slightly more textured, sound a bit deeper, and resist that shine far longer, which is why a lot of enthusiast boards ship with PBT by default. Neither is objectively better in every respect — some people specifically like the lighter, slightly hollower sound of ABS — but PBT is the more common recommendation for anyone buying a board they expect to use daily for years.

Keycap profile, the shape and height of the caps across the rows, also changes typing feel more than people expect. Taller, more sculpted profiles can feel more precise to reach but add height that matters for wrist comfort, while lower, flatter profiles suit people who prefer to type with their hands closer to the desk. This is a separate decision from switch type entirely, and mixing an unfamiliar profile with a new switch type at the same time makes it harder to tell which change is responsible for however the keyboard ends up feeling.

Ergonomics beyond the switch

For anyone typing for long stretches of the day, keyboard shape can matter as much as switch choice. Standard rectangular layouts are the most common and the easiest to get used to, but split and angled layouts — where the keyboard is divided into two halves that can be positioned at shoulder width — are worth considering for anyone experiencing wrist or shoulder discomfort during long sessions. Tenting, where each half is angled inward, reduces wrist rotation further still. These are bigger adjustments than picking a switch colour, and they come with a real learning curve, but for people who type for a living, the ergonomic layout often matters more to long-term comfort than the switch feel discussed throughout the rest of this comparison.

A note on rollover and anti-ghosting

Most mechanical keyboards, regardless of switch type, support full N-key rollover, meaning every key pressed simultaneously registers correctly, which matters for gaming inputs that require several keys held down at once. Cheaper membrane keyboards often can't do this reliably and will "ghost," silently failing to register a keypress when too many keys are held together. This isn't a switch-specific property — it comes down to how the keyboard's internal matrix is wired — but it's worth confirming on any board being considered for gaming, since it's a real functional difference and not just a marketing checkbox.

Is RGB backlighting worth caring about?

Backlighting has no effect on switch feel and doesn't belong in the same decision as linear versus tactile versus clicky, but it's worth a short mention since it's frequently bundled into the same purchase. Per-key RGB lighting is now common even on mid-range boards and adds negligible cost at this point, so it's rarely a reason to choose one keyboard over another on its own. Where it does matter practically is legibility in low light and, for some buyers, the ability to colour-code specific keys for a workflow or game — genuinely useful in a few cases, purely cosmetic in most others, and not something worth letting sway a decision that should really be driven by switch feel and layout.

Common misconceptions worth retiring

A louder click does not mean a more accurate or more responsive keypress — actuation happens at a fixed point in the travel regardless of whether that point makes noise. A switch is not "better" in any universal sense; Red, Blue, and Brown are different tools built for different situations, and the fastest typist in the world can be perfectly happy on any of the three depending on habit and preference. And a switch rating on a spec sheet describes the switch in isolation — the keycaps, case, plate, and stabilisers on the finished board can change the real-world feel enough that two keyboards using the identical switch can still feel noticeably different to type on.

Budget tiers and where the money goes

Entry-level mechanical keyboards, often using Gateron or Kailh switches under a plastic case, deliver most of the core mechanical-switch experience — real actuation feedback, N-key rollover, a switch feel you can identify by name — at a fraction of the cost of enthusiast boards. What that price typically skips is stabiliser tuning, so rattle on the space bar and enter key is common at this tier even though the switches themselves are perfectly fine. Mid-range boards usually add better stabilisers, PBT keycaps, and sometimes hot-swap sockets, which is where a lot of buyers find the best balance between cost and day-to-day feel. Higher-end and custom boards move the money into case material, acoustic foam dampening, gasket-mounted plates that add a slight cushioned bounce to every keystroke, and factory-lubed switches — refinements that matter most to people who've already spent enough time typing on a mechanical board to notice them.

A reasonable rule of thumb: if this is a first mechanical keyboard, a mid-range hot-swappable board is a safer purchase than either the cheapest option or a premium custom build, because it leaves room to try different switches later without buying an entirely new keyboard.

Maintenance and how long switches actually last

Cherry rates its switches for tens of millions of keystrokes, and in practice, switch failure is rare compared to other points of failure on a keyboard — a worn-out USB cable, a cracked case, or a stabiliser that's rattled loose tend to show up long before a switch itself wears out. Day-to-day maintenance is mostly about keeping debris out from under the keycaps: compressed air and an occasional keycap removal for cleaning cover most of what a board needs. Spilled liquids are the more realistic threat to longevity than mechanical wear, so a keyboard with a splash-resistant design, or at minimum removable keycaps and a case that can be opened and dried out, is worth considering for anyone who works near a coffee cup.

Bottom line: linear switches like Red suit fast, repetitive gaming input with minimal resistance; tactile switches like Brown suit mixed typing and gaming without excessive noise; clicky switches like Blue give the most physical and audible feedback but are genuinely loud in shared spaces. Switch feel is a personal preference more than a performance ranking — trying before buying, or choosing a hot-swappable board, removes most of the guesswork.

Frequently asked questions

Which Cherry MX switch is best for gaming?

MX Red is the most commonly recommended for fast-paced, competitive gaming because of its smooth, consistent linear travel, though many players do perfectly well on Brown switches too.

Are Blue switches too loud for an office?

For most open offices and shared spaces, yes — the audible click is noticeable several desks away. Red or Brown switches are the more office-friendly choice.

Can I try different switches without buying several keyboards?

Yes — a hot-swappable keyboard lets you install different switch types in the same board without soldering, which is the most practical way to compare switches firsthand.

Do Gateron or Kailh switches feel the same as Cherry MX?

Close, but not identical. They're built to a similar spec and use the same colour naming, but many enthusiasts find Gateron switches smoother out of the box, while Cherry's are often described as more consistent between individual units.

How long do mechanical switches last before wearing out?

Cherry MX switches are rated for tens of millions of keystrokes, which for almost anyone translates to many years of regular use. Cases, cables, and stabilisers tend to show wear or fail well before the switches themselves do.

Practical checklist before buying

  • Decide how much noise your environment can tolerate before picking a switch family.
  • Consider a hot-swappable board if you're unsure which switch feel suits you.
  • Check keycap material (PBT vs ABS) separately from switch type — it changes sound and texture noticeably.
  • Confirm the physical layout (full-size, tenkeyless, 65%) matches your desk space and habits.
  • If buying wireless, check real input-latency reports for gaming use specifically.
