Comparing Mechanical Keyboard Switches

A practical look at how the three most common mechanical switch types actually feel and perform.

1. Cherry MX Red - Smooth and Linear

Cherry MX Red switches are considered the standard for fast, repeated key presses. They're "linear," meaning there's no bump or click as you press down - the key just moves smoothly from top to bottom with a consistent, gradually increasing resistance.

In terms of feel, the switch starts light (around 30cN of force) and needs about 45cN of pressure to actually register a key press, at 2mm of travel. If you press all the way down to the bottom (4mm), resistance builds up to about 65cN. Because that resistance stays consistent throughout, your fingers don't tire out as quickly - which is part of why competitive gamers like these switches so much.

In our testing, Red switches held up really well under rapid, repeated key presses. Since there's no mechanical bump to reset, you can hit the same key again and again without any lag between presses - great for fast-paced games where every millisecond counts.

Laboratory Engineering Insight: The Linear Efficiency Curve

They're also mechanically simple, which means less wear over time - these switches are typically rated for over 100 million presses.

That smoothness comes with a tradeoff for typing, though. Since there's no physical bump to tell you a key registered, typists often have to rely on watching the screen or pressing all the way down to be sure - which can lead to occasional typos, since the light activation force means small, accidental movements can register too. So while Reds are great for gaming, typists switching to them may need some time to adjust.

The sound they make comes entirely from the key bottoming out, giving a deep, low thud rather than a click. You can quiet this further with dampening rings, which makes Red switches a solid choice if you're in a shared space or streaming late at night.

2. Cherry MX Blue - Tactile and Clicky

Cherry MX Blue switches work differently - they use a two-piece stem designed to give you a clear physical and audible signal when a key registers. As you press down, an inner slider pushes against a metal contact leaf, and once you hit the 2.2mm actuation point, it snaps down with a sharp, audible click.

This creates a distinctive feel: resistance builds up to about 60cN right before the click, then drops to 50cN the moment it registers. That sudden drop tells your finger the keypress went through, so you can move on to the next key without pressing all the way down.

Our long-term testing shows this tactile feedback genuinely helps typing accuracy - writers and programmers can settle into a steady rhythm since the click confirms every keystroke, which helps avoid missed letters during long writing sessions.

That same design that makes Blues great for typing can slow you down in fast gaming. Because the switch has to reset before you can press it again (around 1.5mm back up), rapid double-taps or quick repeated inputs can feel slightly delayed compared to a linear switch.

The other tradeoff is noise - Blue switches are genuinely loud. That's fine at home, but can be distracting in an open office, a library, or a shared living space, so it's worth thinking about your surroundings before choosing them.

3. Cherry MX Brown - A Middle Ground

Cherry MX Brown switches try to split the difference - a subtle bump partway through the keystroke gives you tactile feedback, but without the loud click of the Blue switch.

The feel starts out linear at around 30cN, builds to a small tactile bump around 55cN at 1.2mm, then eases back to about 45cN at the 2mm actuation point. That small bump gives you a quiet physical cue that the key registered, without any loud noise.

For anyone who splits their time between work and gaming on the same keyboard, Browns are a solid all-around choice - responsive enough for quick double-taps in games, while still giving typists enough feedback to type accurately.

Laboratory Engineering Insight: Multi-Role Integration Value

In our testing, Browns worked well as a dependable everyday option for people who don't want to commit fully to either extreme.

That said, since they're trying to do a bit of everything, dedicated gamers might find the small bump slightly distracting during intense play compared to a pure linear switch, and some typists feel the bump is a bit too subtle or "mushy" compared to a proper click.

Still, the moderate noise level makes Browns a genuinely good pick for offices - you get a satisfying mechanical feel without bothering the people around you.
