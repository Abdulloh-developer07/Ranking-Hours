Why this site exists

Most comparison articles online are two spec sheets pasted side by side with a headline slapped on top. That tells you one phone's chip scores higher, or one car's battery is a few kWh bigger, but it rarely tells you which one you'd actually be glad you bought six months later. Ranking Hours started from that gap — the space between what a spec sheet says and what a product is actually like to live with, day after day, once the excitement of a new purchase has worn off.

We're not a testing lab with a dozen engineers and a warehouse of review units. What we do is read the manufacturer documentation closely, then cross-check it against independent professional reviews and long-term owner reports, specifically looking for the places where the number on paper and the real experience start to disagree — because that gap is usually the most useful part of any comparison. Where we have used a product first-hand, the article says so directly rather than leaving it ambiguous.

We also try to say when we're not sure. If two products are genuinely close for most buyers, the article says so instead of manufacturing a decisive winner to make a tidier headline. A comparison that pretends to more certainty than it actually has isn't doing the reader any favours.

The categories we cover — smartphones, laptops, cars, home appliances, audio, gaming and software — aren't arbitrary. Each one is a category where people commonly find themselves choosing between two specific, named products rather than browsing broadly, which is exactly the situation a head-to-head comparison is built to help with. As the site grows, we'd rather go deeper into these seven than spread thin across a dozen loosely related categories with only one or two comparisons in each.

How the coverage has grown

1

Started with phones and laptops

The earliest comparisons focused on smartphones and laptops, partly because those are the categories where the largest number of people are actively deciding between two specific products at any given time, and partly because manufacturer documentation for them is unusually thorough and easy to verify.

2

Added vehicles and home appliances

Cars and EVs came next, followed by home appliances — both categories where the gap between the advertised figures and the real-world experience tends to be larger than most spec sheets let on, which made them a natural fit for the kind of comparison we wanted to write.

3

Rounded out with audio, gaming and software

Audio, gaming, and software and cloud tools filled out the current seven categories, chosen because each has its own specific set of trade-offs — comfort and codec support for audio, exclusive libraries for gaming, pricing tiers for software — that don't map cleanly onto the categories that came before.

4

What's next

Rather than adding an eighth category for its own sake, the current focus is on deepening the seven we already have — more comparisons within cars and EVs in particular, since reader interest there has consistently outpaced the number of articles available, and a few software categories readers have specifically asked us to cover.

What actually earns your trust here

None of this is complicated on its own — the hard part is doing it consistently across dozens of comparisons instead of just the ones we happen to care most about. The four points below are the ones we actually get held to.

Look past the spec sheet

A number on a product page only matters once you know how it plays out in daily use — that's the part we spend the most time on.

Stay neutral about the winner

We don't adjust a conclusion to favour a brand we have an affiliate relationship with, and we say when two products are simply close.

Keep pages current

A comparison gets revisited when a product is discontinued, replaced, or meaningfully repriced, not left to go stale indefinitely.

Actually read feedback

Corrections and category requests sent through the contact page get read, and they regularly change what we publish next.

Research & Comparisons

Comparisons on this site are researched and written by a small editorial team rather than a single named reviewer. We work from manufacturer specifications, publicly available documentation, and independent professional and owner reviews, and we don't claim first-hand hands-on testing on an article unless that testing actually happened. We keep individual contributors unnamed for now rather than attach names to a workflow that's genuinely a team effort across research, writing and fact-checking — if you need to reach us directly about a specific article, the Contact page goes straight to the team responsible for it. Most comparisons pass through at least two people before publishing: one drafting the initial research and comparison structure, and a second doing a fact-check pass against the original sources before it goes live.

How a comparison actually gets made

1

Start from what's officially published

Every comparison begins with the specifications and pricing each manufacturer publishes directly, since that's the one source both products share and the most reliable starting point.

2

Cross-check against independent sources

We read through professional reviews and long-term owner feedback on forums and retailer sites, looking specifically for where the spec sheet and the lived experience disagree.

3

Identify what actually differs day to day

Not every spec difference matters equally. We try to separate the ones that change daily use — battery drain, noise, learning curve — from the ones that are mostly marketing.

4

Write toward "best for what," not just "best"

Where possible, the conclusion names who each product actually suits, rather than forcing a single universal winner onto every kind of buyer.

5

Revisit when something changes

A comparison is updated when a product is discontinued, replaced, or repriced, and gets a slower periodic check even without a specific trigger, since not every stale detail gets reported by a reader before someone else stumbles across it.

Editorial independence, in practice

No brand reviews articles before publishing

Manufacturers and retailers don't see a comparison before it goes live, and none has editorial sign-off over what the article concludes. If a company reaches out after publication to dispute a factual point, we'll check the claim against our sources and correct the page if they're right — but the request itself doesn't guarantee a change.

Affiliate links are added after the conclusion, not before

The comparison and its recommendation are written first, based on the criteria described in the article. Once that's settled, we add an affiliate link to whichever retailer happens to stock the recommended product — the presence or absence of a commission doesn't feed back into which product we recommend.

Sponsored content, if it ever exists, will say so

At the time of writing, nothing published on this site is paid placement. If that changes in the future, the specific article will carry a clear label saying so — sponsorship disclosure won't be something you have to dig for in a footer or a separate policy page.

Reader corrections outrank internal convenience

When a reported error would require reworking a comparison's structure rather than just swapping a number, we still make the change — a comparison that's easy to maintain but factually wrong isn't something we're willing to leave live for the sake of convenience.

What we hold ourselves to

Accuracy

Specifications are checked against the source at the time of publication, and corrected promptly when a reader flags something wrong — a claim that can't be traced back to a manufacturer page or a credible independent review doesn't make it into an article.

Transparency

We distinguish published research from first-hand use, and disclose affiliate relationships rather than burying them in fine print — if we haven't personally used a product, the article won't imply that we have.

Neutrality

A comparison's conclusion is decided by the criteria in the article, not by which retailer happens to be an affiliate partner, and that holds even when the more profitable product for us isn't the one we'd actually recommend.

Practicality

We'd rather explain what a spec means for daily use than repeat a number the manufacturer already published clearly enough — a paragraph that just restates the spec sheet in different words isn't adding anything for the reader.

Timeliness

Comparisons involving a discontinued or heavily repriced product are flagged or updated rather than left to quietly mislead readers who don't realise the article predates a change in the market.

Questions people ask us about the site

Are you affiliated with any manufacturer?

No. We're an independent publisher, and no brand controls or reviews our comparisons before they go live. Affiliate commerce links, where they exist, are disclosed on the affiliate disclosure page.

Do you buy every product you compare?

Not always — see the editorial team note above. We're upfront about when a comparison is built from research rather than hands-on testing, instead of implying testing that didn't happen.

How do you make money?

Mainly display advertising and affiliate commissions when a reader buys through a linked retailer after clicking from this site, at no extra cost to them. Neither influences which product a comparison recommends.

Can I write for Ranking Hours?

We occasionally work with outside contributors on specific categories. The contact page is the right place to reach out with relevant experience or writing samples.

Why don't you name individual writers?

Because most comparisons genuinely pass through more than one person before publishing — research, drafting and fact-checking aren't always done by the same contributor, so attaching a single byline would overstate any one person's involvement rather than accurately reflect how the article was actually put together.

What happens if I find an error?

Send specifics through the contact page — which comparison, which claim, and ideally a source for the correct figure. Genuine errors get fixed; we'd rather have a slightly slower publishing pace than leave something factually wrong live because no one flagged it. We aim to acknowledge correction requests within a few days, even if the actual fix takes a little longer to research properly.

How we handle mistakes

1

A reader flags something

Most corrections start with a message through the contact page — a wrong spec, an outdated price, a link that no longer works, or a conclusion that doesn't match what someone experienced buying the product themselves.

2

We check it against the source

Before changing anything, we go back to the manufacturer documentation or the original review the claim was based on, since a reader's report and our original research occasionally both need re-checking rather than one automatically overriding the other.

3

The page gets updated, not quietly replaced

When a correction changes a meaningful conclusion — not just a typo — we update the article directly rather than publishing a new page and leaving the old, wrong one findable elsewhere on the site.

4

Recurring issues change our process

If the same kind of error keeps coming up across a category — say, EV range figures going stale faster than we'd assumed — that's a signal to check that whole category more often, not just fix the one page.

What we deliberately avoid

Fabricated testimonials

We won't invent quotes from happy customers to make the site look more trusted than the track record actually supports — if we don't have a real, verifiable reader quote, we simply don't publish one.

Undisclosed sponsorship

No comparison on this site exists because a brand paid for placement without saying so. If that arrangement ever changes for a specific article, it will be disclosed clearly on that page, not buried in a footer.

Unsupported superlatives

"Best ever" and "the only one you'll ever need" show up a lot in this space. We avoid that language unless a specific claim behind it can actually be checked against a source.

Silently outdated pages

When a comparison involves a discontinued or heavily repriced product and we haven't gotten to a full rewrite yet, we'd rather flag that plainly than leave the page looking current when it isn't.

Who this site is genuinely useful for

You've narrowed it to two options

If you're already deciding between two specific, named products, a head-to-head comparison is exactly the format that helps — that's the situation every article here is written for.

You want the "why," not just a score

If a single star rating or a "top 10" list from elsewhere feels too thin to actually decide on, our longer-form comparisons are built to explain the reasoning behind a recommendation, not just deliver one.

Who might be better served elsewhere

You need a live price-tracking tool

We mention pricing context, but we're not a real-time price comparison engine — a dedicated price-tracking site will serve that specific need better than we can.

You're browsing broadly, not comparing two things

If you haven't narrowed your options at all yet, a broader buying guide or a retailer's filterable catalogue is probably a faster starting point than a head-to-head comparison. Come back once you've got it down to two or three names — that's when a comparison actually earns its length.

What a typical comparison looks like

An upfront summary

Most articles open with a short, direct answer to "which one, for whom" before the detailed breakdown, since not every reader has time to read the full comparison before deciding.

A side-by-side spec section

The core published specifications sit in one place, sourced directly from the manufacturers, so you can check the raw numbers without wading through prose to find them.

A category-by-category breakdown

Camera, battery, build quality, and whatever else is relevant to that specific product type get their own short section, each ending with which product has the edge and why.

A final recommendation by use case

Rather than one blanket winner, the closing section usually names two or three reader profiles and which product suits each one better.

How we try to write these

Plenty of comparison sites lean on the same handful of stock phrases — "packs a punch," "seamless experience," "elevates your setup" — until every article on every site starts to sound like the same press release. We try actively to avoid that, partly for readability and partly because vague enthusiasm doesn't actually tell you anything a spec sheet didn't already say. A sentence like "the battery lasts noticeably longer on a video call than the competitor's" is more useful than "impressive battery life," even though the second one is shorter and easier to write.

We also try to write for someone who's genuinely deciding, not for someone browsing for entertainment. That means naming actual trade-offs — this one is quieter but that one is cheaper to maintain — rather than padding an article with general category background the reader probably already knows. If a comparison feels shorter than others in the same category, it's usually because there was less genuine daylight between the two products to write about, not because we ran out of things to say.

A couple more questions

Do you cover products outside your seven categories?

Occasionally, if there's clear reader interest and we can research it to the same standard as everything else on the site — but we won't stretch into a category just to look broader than we actually are.

Is Ranking Hours the same team behind other comparison sites?

No — this is a standalone, independently run publication. If you've seen similar-sounding content elsewhere, it isn't from the same editorial team, and we can't speak to how another site's process works or vouch for its accuracy.

What's worth writing to us about

Factual errors

A wrong spec, an outdated price, a discontinued product still listed as current — these are the fastest kind of correction to make, and we genuinely want to know about them.

Broken links

Retailer links occasionally break when a product page moves or a listing expires. Flagging these helps every reader who visits after you, not just you.

Comparison requests

If you're stuck choosing between two specific products we haven't covered yet, tell us. Requests like this directly shape what gets researched next.

Disagreement with a conclusion

If you owned both products in a comparison and reached a different conclusion than we did, that's useful too — it doesn't automatically change the article, but it does get read, considered, and occasionally leads to a follow-up question back to you for more detail.

Have a correction, a request, or a question?

We read everything that comes through the contact page, and it genuinely shapes what gets published and corrected next. If you're weighing two specific products right now and can't find a comparison for them yet, that message is exactly the kind of thing that moves up our research list.
