Electric sedan comparison

BMW i4 or Tesla Model 3: two very different ways to go electric

One is a traditional luxury car that happens to be electric. The other is a software-first car that happens to have four wheels. Which idea suits you decides most of this comparison.

Updated September 28, 2026

BMW i4

Luxury liftback, rear or all-wheel drive

Tesla Model 3

Compact sedan, rear or all-wheel drive

The short version

The Model 3 costs less, goes further per kilowatt-hour, and has a charging network that has been the performance score for years. The i4 is quieter, rides better, feels more finished inside, and keeps the physical buttons and dials that a lot of drivers miss the moment they get in a Tesla. If your main question is "how much car and how much range for the money," the Tesla usually wins. If it's "which one will I enjoy sitting in for two hours," the BMW has a real argument.

Both cars come in several versions, and prices and range figures shift from model year to model year and from country to country. The numbers below are approximate and taken from manufacturer and published test figures, so check the current spec for the exact version you're looking at before you commit. This page is research-based, not a bench test by our team.

Range and how far you really get

Official range numbers are a useful starting point and a poor promise. Both cars are rated in the same broad territory, with the Model 3 Long Range usually listed a bit higher than most i4 versions. The reason is mostly efficiency: the Tesla is lighter and has a slippery shape, so it uses fewer kilowatt-hours per mile. On motorway trips at 70 mph and above, that efficiency gap tends to show up as a few extra stops or a few fewer stops over a long day.

Cold weather hurts every electric car, and neither one is immune. Owners in colder climates commonly report losing a fifth to a third of rated range in deep winter, especially on short trips where the cabin heater does most of the work. Both cars have heat pumps on recent versions, which helps, and both can precondition the battery before a fast-charging stop if you navigate with the built-in system. The Tesla does this a little more smoothly because its navigation is tied directly to the charging stops it plans for you.

The practical takeaway is simple. If you drive under 50 miles on a normal day and charge at home, range is close to irrelevant for either car, and you should decide on other things. If you regularly drive long distances, the extra range and efficiency of the Tesla is a real advantage, though the i4 is far from short on range.

Charging: the network matters as much as the speed

On paper, the Tesla peaks a little higher at a fast charger and holds a good charging curve for a long stretch, so a 10 to 80 percent stop takes in the region of 20 to 30 minutes on a good day. The i4 can pull up to roughly 200 kW and typically manages a similar 10 to 80 percent stop in about half an hour. In everyday terms, that difference is small. What differs more is how easy it is to find a working charger.

Tesla built its own fast-charging network first, made it reliable, and made it simple: you plug in and the car handles payment. That reputation is the main reason many people picked a Model 3 over other electric cars for years. The i4 uses the common CCS plug and relies on public networks, which vary a lot by region in reliability, price, and how many working stalls there are at each site. BMW has also worked on gaining access to Tesla's network through adapters in some markets, which changes the picture in places where that is available, so check what applies where you live.

If you can charge at home, most of this section matters much less. A home or workplace charger that fills the battery overnight means public fast charging becomes an occasional road-trip tool, not a daily habit.

Performance and driving feel

Both cars are quicker than their price suggests, because electric motors deliver torque instantly. In rear-wheel-drive form, both cars accelerate from a standstill to 60 mph in roughly the mid-5-second range, while the Model 3 Performance and the i4 M50 drop into the low 3s and mid 3s respectively. In a real traffic situation, either will get to a gap in the road before most other cars can react.

The difference is in character. The i4 rides with more cushion and less road noise, and feels like a proper grand tourer that happens to be silent. Reviewers who have driven both back to back often describe the BMW steering as more natural and its body control as more relaxed on rough roads. The Model 3 is firmer, a bit sharper in tight corners, and can feel busier on poor surfaces, especially on larger wheels. The latest Model 3 has softened this somewhat compared with older versions, though it still sits on the stiffer side.

Braking is a place where personal preference shows up. Both cars use regenerative braking to slow down when you lift off the accelerator. The Tesla leans toward strong one-pedal driving by default, and many owners love it. The BMW lets you choose how strong the regeneration feels through a few settings, including an adaptive mode that adjusts to traffic ahead. If you like a car that behaves like a normal car when you lift off, the BMW is easier to live with. If you like one-pedal driving, the Tesla makes it feel natural.

Inside: two very different ideas of a cabin

This is where the two cars diverge most. The Model 3 has one large screen and almost nothing else. The speedometer, climate settings, navigation, and even the mirror adjustment are all handled on the center display. Newer versions have moved the turn signals and gear selector into touch controls or steering wheel buttons too. Many owners get used to it within a week and then like the clean look. Others find it distracting, since adjusting the fan speed means looking away from the road.

The i4 keeps a more familiar layout. There is a curved display that combines the instrument cluster and the center screen, but you still get a physical rotary controller between the seats, real buttons for the most common functions, and a proper gear selector. The materials feel more expensive: better leather or leather-like surfaces, more metal-look trim, and tighter panel fit. The Tesla interior is pleasant and airy, with a big glass roof, but it feels simpler and plainer in comparison.

Space and everyday practicality

The i4's hatchback design makes it more useful for awkward loads than the Model 3's conventional trunk. With the rear seats folded, you can carry long or tall items that would not fit through a sedan trunk opening. The Model 3 counters with a large trunk plus a small front storage area, and its total cargo volume with the seats up is competitive. Rear-seat space is similar in the two, meaning fine for two adults on a normal journey and a little tight for three across.

The i4 is a longer, wider car than the Model 3, which helps with rear legroom on paper and with a sense of stability on the road, but makes it slightly harder to park in tight city spaces. If you live in a dense area with narrow streets, the Tesla's smaller footprint is a small but real convenience.

Driver assistance and software

Tesla's software is the thing many people buy the car for. Over-the-air updates arrive often and sometimes add real features, and the interface is quick and consistent. The standard driver-assistance package handles adaptive cruise and lane keeping well. The optional Full Self-Driving package, sold as a paid upgrade or subscription depending on the market, adds more automated behavior in cities and on highways, but it still requires a fully attentive driver, and regulations and availability differ by country. Treat any marketing that suggests otherwise with caution.

BMW offers its own driver-assistance packages, with a highway assistant that lets you take your hands off the wheel on some approved roads while the car keeps lane and speed. BMW also delivers over-the-air updates, though usually fewer and less frequent than Tesla's. The BMW system feels more conservative and predictable, and the driver monitoring is stricter about paying attention. Tesla's approach feels more ambitious and gets more attention in the press, and it has also been the subject of more regulatory scrutiny.

Phone integration is another quiet difference. The i4 supports Apple CarPlay and Android Auto, so your maps and music appear on the big screen the way you're used to. The Model 3 does not offer either, and depends on Tesla's own navigation and media apps instead. For some buyers this is a non-issue, and for others it is the deciding factor.

Price, trims, and the cost of owning each one

The Model 3 undercuts the i4 by a noticeable margin at the entry level. That price gap is the strongest argument for the Tesla, particularly once you include the fact that it also offers more range for the money. Tesla has also changed its prices several times in recent years, so the number you see today may not match what a friend paid a year ago. The i4 is priced as a premium car and holds to that positioning, with options and packages that add up quickly.

Running costs tend to be lower than for a petrol car on both. Electricity is cheaper per mile than fuel almost everywhere if you charge at home, and both cars need much less routine maintenance than a combustion engine: no oil changes, fewer brake jobs thanks to regeneration, no exhaust to fix. Tires are the real recurring cost, since heavy, powerful electric cars wear them out faster than you might expect. Insurance is a variable to check before buying, as premiums for both models can differ a lot by region and driver profile.

Depreciation is harder to predict. Tesla's price cuts have put pressure on used values in some markets, and used Model 3 prices have fallen accordingly. The i4 has generally followed the wider pattern for premium electric cars. If resale value matters to you, look at current used listings for the exact model year, not just the general reputation of the brand.

Warranty, service, and reliability

Both brands offer a basic warranty of about four years and a longer battery warranty of around eight years, with mileage limits that differ by trim and market. Read the paperwork for the exact terms where you live. The bigger difference is in how you get service. BMW uses its established dealer network, which is extensive in most places and comes with the usual loaner-car and courtesy options. Tesla operates its own service centers and a mobile-service fleet that can handle small repairs at your home or office, though wait times for appointments and parts have varied by location.

Reliability data for both cars is mixed and depends on the year. Early Model 3 production was known for uneven panel gaps and paint issues, and later years have improved a lot. Owner surveys in different countries put both cars around the middle of the pack for problems, with electric-specific issues, such as infotainment glitches or charging port faults, showing up more than mechanical failures. That's a reason to prefer a car with a clear warranty history and a service center within reasonable distance.

Trims and model years: where people get confused

Both cars have been refreshed in recent years, and that makes online reviews harder to use than they look. A review of an older Model 3 talks about stalks, a softer touchscreen layout, and a different suspension feel that no longer match the current car. The same goes for the i4, which received updates to its screens, its range, and some of its trim structure. When you read any opinion about either car, check the model year first, because comments that were fair in one year can be wrong in the next.

For the BMW, the choice is mostly between the rear-drive eDrive40, the all-wheel-drive xDrive40, and the high-output M50. The eDrive40 is the value pick and the one most reviewers point to as the best balance of range and price. The M50 is much faster, but it costs more, uses more energy, and rides on bigger wheels that make the car feel harder. For the Tesla, the split is between the entry rear-drive version, the Long Range model with a bigger battery and dual motors, and the Performance version with a sportier setup. Most buyers who want a sensible everyday car end up looking at the first two.

Three kinds of driver, three kinds of answer

The daily commuter

If you drive a short route to work, charge at home, and take one or two longer trips a year, the range and charging speed differences barely matter. What matters is comfort, cabin noise, and how much you like the interface you'll touch every day. Here the BMW's quiet ride and buttons tend to win over people who test both, while the Tesla's lower price is the main reason to pick it. A test drive of about half an hour each will tell you more than any table.

The family on weekends

For families, practical things count: how easy it is to get a child seat in and out, how much luggage fits, and how the rear seats feel on a long trip. The i4's hatch opening and folding seats are helpful for strollers and bulky bags, and the taller roofline at the rear gives a bit more space for passengers. The Model 3's large trunk and front storage are also useful, though the trunk opening is narrower. Bring your own child seat to the test drive, since fit varies more than the numbers suggest.

The frequent long-distance driver

This is the group where the Tesla's efficiency and charging network add up most. Fewer stops, more predictable chargers, and a navigation system that plans around them make a long day easier. That said, an i4 driver who plans a route in advance and knows the chargers along it can do the same trip with only a little more effort, and many do it happily.

Buying used instead of new

Used prices for both cars have come down enough that a slightly older example is worth a look. With a used Model 3, check the battery's health report if the seller can provide one, look at the panel alignment and paint, and make sure the version you're getting still has the features you expect, since some were added or removed between years. With a used i4, check that the remaining warranty transfers to you, look at the tire condition, and ask about the service history at a BMW dealer.

In both cases, a battery that has been fast-charged constantly may have aged a little faster than one that was mostly charged at home, though modern batteries hold up better than early fears suggested. A pre-purchase inspection by a shop that is familiar with electric cars is money well spent, especially if the car is out of warranty or close to it.

What owners tend to say after a year

People who've lived with a Model 3 often say the things they like most are the speed of the software, the ease of long trips, and the low running costs. The complaints tend to be about ride firmness, road noise on some surfaces, and the absence of physical controls for simple tasks. People who own an i4 usually praise the quiet, comfortable ride and the feel of the interior, and complain about the price of options, slower software updates, and range that can feel a bit shorter than advertised in winter.

These are general patterns, not a rule, and any one owner's experience can differ. It's worth reading a few long-term owner threads for the specific model year you're looking at, since they tend to reveal the everyday annoyances that a short test drive can miss.

Which one should you get?

There is no universal winner, because the two cars are aimed at different priorities. The Tesla is the sensible choice for someone who wants the most range and charging convenience per dollar, likes a minimal cabin, and is comfortable with software doing most of the work. The BMW is the better fit for someone who wants a more traditional, more refined car, who values physical controls, quiet comfort, and phone mirroring, and who is willing to pay more for that feel.

Pick the Model 3 ifyou want the lowest price, longest range, and the smoothest charging experience, and you're fine with a screen-centered cabin.
Pick the i4 ifyou care about ride comfort, cabin quality, physical controls, and CarPlay, and you can accept a higher price.

Common questions before choosing

Which one is cheaper to run?

The Model 3 usually uses less electricity per mile, so it tends to cost a little less to run. The difference is small next to the price gap between the two cars.

Can I use Apple CarPlay in a Model 3?

No. The Model 3 relies on Tesla's own system, while the i4 supports CarPlay and Android Auto.

Which is better for long road trips?

The Model 3 has an edge in range and the strength of its charging network in many regions. The i4 counters with a calmer, quieter ride that some drivers find less tiring over a full day.

Is it worth waiting for a newer version?

Both models get regular updates. If you are buying soon, focus on the version available now, and check for current incentives or price changes before you decide.
