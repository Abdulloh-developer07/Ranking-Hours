Laptop Comparisons and Buying Guide

What really matters when you choose a laptop, explained in plain words, with links to our full MacBook, Windows, business and gaming comparisons.

Last updated September 30, 2026

Start here

Buying a laptop is harder than it should be. There are hundreds of models, the names are confusing, and two machines that look alike can feel completely different after a week of use. The good news is that you can ignore most of the noise if you answer a few simple questions first.

What will you do most of the time? Where will you use it? How long should it last? And how much can you spend without feeling sick about it? Once you have those four answers, the choice usually shrinks from hundreds of laptops to a handful.

This page is the starting point for our laptop guides. It explains what actually matters in a laptop, in plain words, and then sends you to the full comparisons for the big decisions: MacBook or Windows, thin and light or powerful, business or gaming. Read it once, and the other pages will make a lot more sense.

A note on how we work. We base our guides on published specifications, the makers' documentation and public information. We do not claim to have tested every laptop, and we never invent performance score numbers. Prices and exact configurations change all the time, so we leave them out and ask you to check the store page for the exact model before you pay.

The five questions that decide everything

1. What is the main job?

Writing, browsing, studying and video calls need very little power. Photo and video editing need more. Games, 3D work and some engineering software need a lot. Be honest here, because paying for power you never use is the most common waste in laptop buying.

2. Where will you use it?

A laptop that lives on a desk can be big and heavy, with a large screen and plenty of ports. A laptop that goes to school or on trips every day should be light, with a battery that lasts. Many people buy a heavy laptop and then leave it at home.

3. How long should it last?

If you want four or five years of use, spend a little more on memory and storage now. These are the parts that make an older machine feel slow, and on many models you cannot change them later.

4. What system do you need?

Some work depends on Windows, some on macOS. If you are not sure, read Windows 11 vs macOS before you look at any laptop.

5. What is the real budget?

Include a case, a mouse, an adapter and maybe extra warranty. Then decide on a number and stick to it. A laptop that is a little cheaper and does the job is better than a stretch that leaves you stressed.

The parts that matter

You do not need to become an expert. These are the main parts, and what to look for in each.

Processor

This is the brain. Laptops use chips from Intel, AMD, Apple and Qualcomm. For everyday use, any recent mid-range chip is fine. What matters more than the brand name is how new it is and how the laptop handles heat. A strong chip in a thin body can slow down when it gets hot, so independent reviews are useful here. Some Windows laptops now use Arm-based chips, which can give long battery life, but a few older programs may not run well on them. Check your key programs before you buy one.

Memory (RAM)

Memory is the short-term workspace. With too little, a laptop gets slow when you open many tabs and programs. For light use, 8 GB can still work, but 16 GB is a safer choice if you want the laptop to stay comfortable for years. If you edit video, work with big files or run virtual machines, think about more. Check whether the memory can be upgraded, because on many thin laptops it cannot.

Storage

Almost all modern laptops use SSD storage, which is fast and quiet. The question is size. 256 GB fills up quickly with photos, downloads and updates. 512 GB is a sensible starting point for most people, and more if you keep videos or games on the machine. External drives and cloud storage help, but they are not as handy as space on the laptop itself.

Screen

You look at the screen all day, so it deserves attention. Check the size, the resolution, the brightness and the type of panel. A bright, sharp screen with good colours is a joy. A dim one is tiring, especially near a window. If you travel or work outdoors, brightness matters a lot. For photo and design work, look for good colour coverage. For games, a higher refresh rate makes motion look smoother.

Battery

The maker's battery claim is a best case, not a promise. Look for independent tests that show real use, like web browsing and video. Battery life also depends on screen brightness and what you run. If you need to work away from sockets, treat this as a top priority.

Keyboard and touchpad

People forget these until they type a long essay on a bad keyboard. If you can, try the keys in a shop. A good keyboard has a clear press and sensible spacing. A good touchpad is large, smooth and accurate. These parts are hard to judge from a spec sheet.

Ports

Thin laptops sometimes offer very few ports. Think about what you plug in: a monitor, a mouse, a memory card, a printer. A small adapter solves most problems, but it is one more thing to carry. USB-C ports differ, so check which ones support charging, displays and fast data.

MacBook or Windows laptop?

This is the first big fork in the road, and our guide on MacBook vs Windows laptop goes through it step by step. In short, MacBooks are known for good screens, strong battery life and a tidy system, and they work especially well with an iPhone. Windows laptops give you far more choice of shapes, prices and brands, and they are the main option if you play PC games or need Windows-only software.

If you are looking at thin, premium machines on both sides, our MacBook vs ultrabook page compares them directly. It explains where an Apple laptop and a premium Windows ultrabook are really different, and where they are close.

Business laptops: Dell and Lenovo

Business laptops are built for people who type a lot, travel often and need the machine to keep working. They tend to have good keyboards, solid hinges, useful ports and options for security and long warranties. Two of the best-known families are Dell's XPS and Latitude lines and Lenovo's ThinkPad range.

If you are comparing them, read Dell XPS vs Lenovo ThinkPad. We look at what each family is known for and which kind of user tends to prefer which. Remember that model lines change every year, so check the current version of any laptop you like.

Gaming laptops

A gaming laptop packs a powerful graphics chip and a strong processor into a portable body. That power brings costs: more weight, louder fans, more heat and shorter battery life when you are not plugged in.

The most important part is the graphics chip, because it decides how well games run. Cooling matters almost as much. A powerful chip in a laptop with weak cooling will slow itself down to stay safe. Screen refresh rate, keyboard feel and the number of ports also affect daily life.

We compare popular families in ASUS ROG vs MSI and in the wider gaming laptop comparison. If you want more power per money and do not need to move around, also look at our PC build guide, because a desktop usually gives more speed for the same price.

Memory, storage and the upgrade trap

Many new laptops have memory soldered to the main board. That means the amount you buy is the amount you keep. Storage is sometimes upgradeable and sometimes not.

This changes how you should shop. If you are buying a laptop with fixed memory, lean toward the larger option, even if it costs more. The extra cost at the start is almost always smaller than the cost of replacing the whole laptop two years early.

If you are looking at a model with upgradeable parts, you have more freedom. You can buy a lower configuration and add memory or a bigger drive later, often cheaper than the shop's upgrade price. Search for the exact model name and the word "upgrade" to see what other owners say.

Size, weight and carrying it around

Screen size changes the whole feeling of a laptop. Small screens around 13 inches are easy to carry and fit on a crowded desk. Mid sizes around 14 inches are a popular balance. Larger screens of 15 and 16 inches give you more room for spreadsheets, editing and games, but the machine gets heavier and the charger usually gets bigger too.

Look at the weight in the spec sheet, then add the charger. A laptop that weighs a little over a kilogram feels light in a bag. One that weighs more than two kilograms starts to feel heavy after a long walk. If you carry it every day, this matters more than any performance score.

Think about the shape too. Thin bodies look nice but may have fewer ports and less cooling. Thicker bodies can feel less elegant but often have better keyboards, more ports and cooler running.

Webcam, microphone and speakers

If you spend a lot of time in video calls, these parts matter more than people expect. Many laptops still have a weak webcam, and some budget models have a very basic one. A sharper camera and a decent microphone make you look and sound better in meetings, and they save you from buying extra gear.

Speakers vary a lot. Some laptops sound surprisingly good, with clear voices and a little bass. Others sound thin and tinny. If you watch films or listen to music on the laptop without headphones, listen to it in a shop first. If you use headphones most of the time, you can ignore this and look at our headphone guide instead.

Also check for a privacy shutter on the camera, and for a way to turn off the microphone by key. They are small things that give you peace of mind.

How to read laptop reviews

Reviews are useful, but you have to read them with care.

First, check which exact model the review covers. The same laptop name can come with different chips, screens and memory, and a review of the top version may not describe the cheaper one you are about to buy.

Second, look for measurements instead of adjectives. Real battery tests, brightness readings and noise levels tell you more than words like "fast" or "premium". Be wary when a review gives only praise and no weak points. Every laptop has some.

Third, read what owners say after some months. Early reviews cover the first week. Problems with hinges, battery wear, coil noise and software show up later. Owner discussions are not always fair, but a pattern of the same complaint is worth noticing.

Finally, notice who benefits. If a site earns money from a link, the review may lean toward the product. That is true of many sites, including ours when we use affiliate links, which we explain on the disclosure page. Compare several sources before you decide.

Warranty and repair

A laptop is a machine you carry around, so it will get knocked, spilled on and dropped at some point. Think about repair before you need it.

Check how long the warranty lasts and what it covers. Some cover only manufacturing faults, not accidents. Ask where service is done. If the nearest service centre is in another city, that changes what a problem costs you in time.

Think about how repairable the laptop is. Some models let a technician replace a keyboard, battery or storage in minutes. Others are glued together and need a full part replaced. Widely sold models from big brands usually have spare parts and local technicians more easily than unusual ones.

Extended warranty can be worth it for an expensive laptop that you cannot afford to lose, but read what it actually covers and what you must do to claim. Keep your receipt and serial number in a safe place.

A simple shopping routine

If all of this feels like a lot, follow this short routine.

  • Write down your main tasks, your budget and your system choice.
  • Pick three laptops that fit, using our comparison pages as a guide.
  • Read the specifications of each, with a focus on memory, storage, screen and weight.
  • Search for independent reviews and owner comments for the exact model.
  • Try the keyboard and screen in a shop if you can.
  • Compare the real price and warranty from two or three sellers.
  • Buy, then back up your files and update the system on day one.

This takes an evening, and it will save you from the kind of regret that lasts for years.

Looking after the battery and the machine

A good laptop can last many years if you treat it kindly. A few habits make a real difference.

  • Do not leave it on a bed or sofa while it works hard. Soft surfaces block the vents.
  • Avoid very hot places, like a car in summer. Heat shortens battery life more than almost anything.
  • If you keep it plugged in for months, use any battery care option the maker provides, such as a charge limit.
  • Clean the vents gently with a soft brush now and then, especially on gaming laptops.
  • Keep the system and drivers updated, and keep a backup of your files.
  • Carry it in a padded case, and do not squeeze it into a crowded bag with sharp objects.

Batteries slowly lose capacity with age, and that is normal. If your battery drops a lot after two or three years, a replacement can make an old laptop feel new again, if the maker or a good repair shop offers it.

Buying new, used or refurbished

A new laptop comes with a full warranty and no surprises. A used one can be a very good deal, especially for business laptops and MacBooks that hold their quality well. A refurbished one sits between the two, if it comes from a trusted seller with a clear warranty.

If you buy used, test it properly. Check the screen for dead pixels and dark patches, the keyboard for every key, the ports, the speakers, the webcam and the battery health. Look at the serial number and ask whether the laptop is locked to an account. Ask for a receipt, and be careful of deals that look too good.

For an older Mac, find out which version of macOS it can still run. For an older Windows laptop, find out if it can run Windows 11, since Windows 10 no longer gets regular security updates. A cheap laptop that cannot be updated is not a bargain.

Real-life examples

A university student

The student needs something light, with a long battery and a good keyboard. A thin laptop with 16 GB of memory and a 512 GB drive is a comfortable base. Gaming power is not needed, unless the course requires it.

A small business owner

The owner works on documents, spreadsheets, calls and email. A business laptop with a sturdy build and good support makes sense. Spending extra on a faster chip brings little, while memory and a reliable keyboard bring a lot.

A video editor

This person needs power, a good screen and fast storage. A strong MacBook or a powerful Windows laptop both work. Cooling and the screen quality matter more than the logo.

A gamer who travels

A gaming laptop is the answer, but the person should accept a heavier bag and a short battery life away from the charger. A strong graphics chip and good cooling should come before fancy lights.

A parent buying for a child

A simple, durable laptop with a decent screen is plenty. Spend on a good warranty and a protective case before spending on speed.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying on the processor name alone. The rest of the laptop decides how it feels every day.
  • Choosing the smallest memory and storage. They cannot always be upgraded, and they age first.
  • Trusting a battery claim. Look for independent tests.
  • Ignoring the keyboard. You will touch it every minute you use the machine.
  • Paying for gaming power you will not use. It adds weight and cost.
  • Forgetting warranty and local repair. A great laptop is not much use if nobody nearby can fix it.

Common questions

How much memory do I need?

For light use, 8 GB can work, but 16 GB is a safer choice if you want the laptop to stay comfortable for years. Heavy editing and development often benefit from more.

Is a more expensive laptop always better?

No. Price often reflects build quality, screen and support, but also brand and size. Match the machine to your tasks, not to the price tag.

Should I buy a touchscreen laptop?

Only if you will use it. Touch is useful for notes and drawing. It can add weight and cost, and it can shorten battery life a little.

How often should I replace a laptop?

Many people keep a good laptop for four to six years. Replace it when it becomes too slow for your work, stops getting security updates or the battery is no longer usable.

Do I need a laptop or a desktop?

If you move around, you need a laptop. If you work at one desk and want more power for the money, a desktop is often better. See our PC build guide.
