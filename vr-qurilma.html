Oculus Quest 2 vs HTC Vive

Standalone headset or PC-based VR? Here is how the two approaches differ, and which one fits your room, your budget and your computer.

Last updated September 30, 2026

The short answer

Virtual reality headsets come in two main families, and understanding the difference saves more money than any feature list. A standalone headset, like the Meta Quest line (once sold under the Oculus name), has the computer built in. You put it on and play. A PC-based headset, like many HTC Vive models, connects to a powerful computer that does the heavy work.

For most people trying VR for the first time, a standalone headset is the easier and cheaper way in. For people who already own a strong gaming PC, want the highest picture quality, or use VR for demanding simulations, a PC-based headset can give more. And many standalone headsets can also connect to a PC, so you do not always have to choose one path forever.

This guide compares the two ideas using the Oculus Quest 2 and the HTC Vive family as our examples. A note on timing: the Quest 2 is an older model and Meta has released newer headsets since, and HTC has several newer Vive products too. We use these as clear examples of the two approaches, and we point out where to check the current lineup.

Our method is the same as everywhere on this site. We rely on published specifications, official documents and public information. We do not claim to have run lab tests, and we do not invent numbers.

How VR actually works

A VR headset puts a screen in front of each eye, with lenses that make the picture fill your view. Sensors track how your head moves, and the picture updates to match, so the world seems to stay still while you look around. Controllers or your hands let you interact.

The key idea is called six degrees of freedom, or 6DoF. It means the headset tracks not only the direction you look, but also where your body moves forward, backward, sideways and up and down. Almost all modern headsets do this. Without it, VR feels flat and can make you feel unwell.

There are two main ways to track movement. Inside-out tracking uses cameras on the headset itself to watch the room. Outside-in tracking uses small boxes placed around the room, often called base stations, that send out signals the headset can read. Inside-out is simple to set up. Outside-in can be very precise but needs more equipment and space.

If you do not own a gaming PC, a PC-based headset means buying two expensive things. If you do own one, you can have the best of both by using a standalone headset with a link cable or wireless link to your computer.

Oculus Quest 2: the easy start

The Quest 2 became popular because it removed the hurdles. It needs no PC, no base stations and no cables in normal use. You set up a safe play area with the headset's cameras, put it on, and open a game.

It has a built-in store with a large library of games and apps, including fitness, social and creative programs. It can also connect to a PC, by cable or wireless link, to play games made for PC headsets. Both of these features make it a flexible first headset.

Its limits are the limits of a small, battery-powered computer. Graphics are good but not as rich as a strong PC can deliver. The battery lasts a couple of hours of active play. The headset is also tied to a Meta account, which matters for privacy and for access to the store. Be sure to read the account terms before you buy.

Because the Quest 2 is an older model, newer Quest headsets offer sharper pictures, better lenses and mixed reality features that blend virtual objects with your room. If you are buying now, compare the current models, and consider the Quest 2 mainly if you find it at a very good price from a trusted seller.

The HTC Vive family: more precision, more setup

HTC was one of the pioneers of consumer VR. The original Vive used base stations around the room and controllers that tracked precisely. That approach gave very accurate movement and a large play area, and it became a favourite among enthusiasts.

Later Vive products include higher-resolution headsets for PC, and standalone models that do not need base stations. The lineup is wider and more varied than Meta's, and it includes models for gaming, for business and for professional work.

The strengths of the PC-based Vive approach are tracking accuracy, access to the PC game library and the ability to use very powerful graphics. The costs are more setup, a powerful computer and sometimes more cables. The right Vive for you depends on whether you want a gaming headset, a professional tool or a standalone device, so check the exact model carefully.

What to look at in the specifications

Resolution

This is the number of pixels per eye. Higher resolution gives a sharper picture and reduces the "screen door" effect, where you can see the gaps between pixels. But resolution is only one part of the story. Lens quality and the screen type also matter.

Refresh rate

Refresh rate is how often the picture updates each second. Higher rates feel smoother and are more comfortable for many people. Common values range from around 72 Hz to 120 Hz or more.

Field of view

This is how wide the picture looks. A wider view feels more immersive. Differences between headsets can be quite noticeable, so look at reviews that discuss it.

Lenses

Different lens designs affect clarity, the size of the sharp area and the thickness of the headset. Newer headsets often use thinner lenses that make the device lighter and more balanced.

Tracking and controllers

Check how well the controllers track, how they feel in your hands, how long their batteries last and whether the headset supports hand tracking, where you use your bare hands instead of controllers.

Audio

Many headsets have built-in speakers close to your ears, and a headphone jack or a way to use your own headphones. Good sound adds a lot to the feeling of being in the experience.

Comfort and fit

A VR headset is something you wear on your face, so comfort decides how long you will play. Weight matters, but so does balance. A headset that is heavy at the front pulls on your face. A well-balanced one with a good strap spreads the load.

Look for an adjustable strap, a soft face pad and an adjustment for the distance between your eyes, called interpupillary distance or IPD. If the lenses are not lined up with your eyes, the picture will look blurry, and you can get a headache. Most headsets offer some adjustment, and some offer more.

If you wear glasses, check that the headset has room for them, or that you can use prescription lens inserts. Many models have a spacer for glasses. Some people also find that a different strap makes a big difference, and several accessory straps are sold for popular headsets.

Heat and sweat are other issues. Active games can make you warm, and a face pad that is easy to clean or replace is a real advantage.

Motion sickness

Some people feel dizzy or sick in VR. It happens when your eyes see movement that your body does not feel. It is more likely in games where you move with a stick while standing still, and less likely when you walk around or stay in one place.

You can reduce the problem. Start with short sessions. Choose games with comfort settings, such as teleport movement and a narrowed view during motion. Keep the room cool, with a fan if needed, because warmth makes it worse. Make sure the headset is adjusted well, since a blurry or misaligned picture adds to the problem. Stop at the first sign of discomfort, and rest.

Many people adapt after a few sessions. Others never fully do, and that is normal. If you are sensitive, ask to try a headset before you buy, or buy from a seller with a clear return policy.

Space and safety

VR is a physical activity, even if you only mean to sit and look around. You cannot see the real room while the headset is on, so safety needs planning.

  • Clear a play area, free of furniture, cables, pets and breakable objects.
  • Use the headset's boundary system to mark the safe area, and respect the warnings.
  • Check the ceiling, lights and fans above you, especially if you swing your arms.
  • Use the wrist straps on controllers.
  • Tell the people at home that you are playing, so no one walks into you.
  • Take breaks, drink water and stop if you feel unwell.

The room does not have to be large. Many games work in a small space. But for games with lots of movement, more space is better. Think of the area as roughly a couple of metres on each side as a minimum, and check each headset's recommendation.

What you can do in VR

VR is not only for games, though games are the biggest part. Here are the main uses.

  • Games. Action, adventure, sports, music and puzzle games, from relaxed to intense.
  • Fitness. Boxing, dance and workout apps that make exercise feel like play. Many people find them a fun way to stay active.
  • Social spaces. Meeting friends as avatars, playing together and attending virtual events.
  • Video and cinema. Watching films on a giant virtual screen, including 3D and 360-degree video.
  • Creativity. Painting and sculpting in 3D space.
  • Learning and training. Education, simulation and professional practice.
  • Work. Virtual monitors and meeting rooms, though comfort limits long use.

Think about which of these you will really use. A headset that gets used a few times and then stays in a drawer is expensive. Games and fitness have the strongest staying power for most people.

The game library and accounts

Content decides how much you will enjoy a headset. Standalone headsets have their own store with many titles made for the device. PC headsets can play games from PC stores, and some of the biggest experiences, like detailed simulations and large adventures, live there.

A standalone headset that links to a PC can often reach both. Check what the link needs, such as a good cable, a fast router for wireless play and a graphics card that meets the requirements.

Accounts matter too. Many headsets need an account with the maker, and sometimes you have to use it to buy games. Think about privacy, parental controls and what happens to your purchases if the account has a problem. Also check which regions are supported, since store access and payment methods can vary by country.

Buying in Uzbekistan

Availability of VR headsets depends on the importer and the market. Some models are sold officially, while others arrive through individual sellers or online orders. This affects the warranty, the power adapter, the language of the setup and your access to the store.

Before you buy, ask about the warranty, who handles repairs and whether the headset is the version made for your region. Check that the charger works with local sockets, or that you have an adapter. Make sure the store and payment methods work for your account. And be careful with very cheap offers, especially for popular models, since fakes and used items sold as new do exist.

If you buy used, test the headset thoroughly. Look at the lenses for scratches, check that the tracking works in all directions, try both controllers and look at the face pad and the strap. Ask whether the account has been reset, because a headset locked to a previous account can be useless.

Children and eye health

Makers set minimum ages for their headsets, and these can change. Read the official safety guidance before you let a child use VR, and supervise them closely. Young children are still developing their vision, and the headset may not fit their face well.

For everyone, take regular breaks. Looking at screens close to your eyes can cause tiredness and strain. If you feel eye pain, headaches or dizziness, stop. If you have a medical condition that affects your balance, vision or heart, ask your doctor before you use VR for long sessions.

These are general points and not medical advice. Always follow the maker's guidance.

Your first evening with a headset

A calm start makes a big difference. Charge the headset and controllers before you begin, and update the software, since a first update is common and can take a while.

Set up your play area with enough light, so the cameras can see the room. Adjust the strap and the eye distance until the picture looks sharp across the centre. Then try a short, gentle experience first, like a tutorial or a relaxed game, for ten or fifteen minutes. Do not jump into a fast roller coaster on the first day.

After the session, notice how you feel. If your eyes are tired or you feel a little dizzy, rest and try again the next day. Add a few more minutes each time. Most people get comfortable within a week.

Playing PC games on a standalone headset

If you own a PC, linking it to a standalone headset opens up a big library. You can connect with a good USB cable, or wirelessly over your home network.

Wireless play depends on your Wi-Fi. A modern router, placed in the same room, with the PC connected by cable to the router, gives the best result. Use a fast Wi-Fi band for the headset, and keep other heavy traffic off the network while you play. If the picture stutters or blurs, move closer to the router, lower the streaming quality or switch to a cable.

The PC needs a graphics card that meets the requirements for VR. Check the official minimum and recommended specifications, and remember that more power gives a smoother and sharper result. Our PC build guide can help you decide.

Accessories worth considering

  • A better strap. Improves balance and comfort on many headsets.
  • A washable or replaceable face cover. Keeps things hygienic and comfortable.
  • Prescription lens inserts. A good choice if you wear glasses and want a clear, safe fit.
  • A charging dock. Keeps the headset and controllers ready.
  • A carry case. Protects the lenses when you travel.
  • Spare batteries for controllers. Small, but you will want them at some point.
  • Headphones. For private, strong sound. See our headphone guide.

Buy accessories from the maker or from reputable sellers. Cheap copies can be uncomfortable, and some can damage the headset.

Looking after the lenses and screens

This is the most important care tip. Never leave a VR headset in direct sunlight, even for a short time. The lenses can focus sunlight onto the screen like a magnifying glass and burn it permanently. Store the headset away from windows and put the cover on the lenses.

Clean the lenses gently with a dry microfibre cloth. Do not rub hard, and do not use liquids unless the maker says so. Wipe the face pad with a suitable cleaner or a damp cloth, and let it dry. Keep the headset in a cool, dry place, and do not drop it.

If the tracking cameras get dirty, wipe them softly too. A smudge can cause the headset to lose track of your position.

Who should choose which

A standalone headset is a good fit if you

  • want to start quickly without a gaming PC
  • like moving freely without cables
  • care about fitness, casual games and social apps
  • have a limited budget for the whole setup

A PC-based headset is a good fit if you

  • already own a powerful gaming PC
  • want the sharpest picture and top accuracy
  • play demanding simulations such as flying or racing
  • need a headset for professional or business use

If you are unsure, a standalone headset with a PC link is the safest middle path.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Buying a PC headset without checking your PC. A weak graphics card gives a poor experience.
  • Ignoring the space. Plan the play area first.
  • Judging by resolution only. Lenses, comfort and tracking matter just as much.
  • Skipping comfort accessories. A better strap or a cleanable face pad can transform a headset.
  • Playing too long on day one. Build up slowly to avoid sickness and strain.
  • Forgetting the account and store region. Check before you pay.

Common questions

Do I need a PC for VR?

No. A standalone headset works without one. A PC is only needed for PC-based headsets, or to play PC games on a standalone headset.

Is the Quest 2 still worth buying?

It can be, at a good price from a trusted seller, but newer headsets offer better pictures and features. Compare current models and the warranty.

Can I wear glasses in VR?

Usually yes, with a spacer or prescription inserts. Check each headset's fit and your glasses' size.

Will VR make me sick?

Some people feel unwell, especially with smooth-motion games. Start slowly, use comfort settings and stop if you feel bad.

Is it safe for children?

Follow the maker's age guidance and supervise. Short sessions and regular breaks matter.
