Toyota RAV4 vs Honda CR-V: which compact SUV should you buy?

Two of the most trusted family SUVs in the world, built for the same job. The gap between them is small, so the details are what matter.

Same job, different personalities

The RAV4 and the CR-V have been rivals for almost thirty years. Both are about the size of a small family estate, with a high seating position, a big boot, an affordable running cost and a reputation for lasting a very long time. If a friend says they are looking for "a sensible SUV that will not give me trouble", these are the two names that come up first.

That similarity makes choosing hard, because there is no obvious winner. It also means you are unlikely to make a bad choice. What separates them is personality. The Toyota feels tougher and more utilitarian, with a bolder shape and a stronger focus on hybrids and rough-road ability. The Honda feels calmer and more refined, with a roomier back seat and a quieter, more grown-up cabin.

Both models have been through new generations in recent years, and Toyota in particular has been pushing the RAV4 further toward hybrid power. Because the exact version for sale depends on your country and the year, we write about the two cars in general terms and point out where a feature depends on the model. We built this page from the makers' published information, the safety ratings of independent test bodies, and what owners and reviewers commonly report. We have not driven these cars for this page, and we do not claim our own test results.

Engines, hybrids and fuel use

Both cars are available with a plain petrol engine and a hybrid, and this is the biggest choice you will make. A hybrid combines a petrol engine with an electric motor and a small battery. You never plug it in, and it recharges itself while you drive and brake. In stop-and-go city traffic it can use far less fuel than a petrol-only car, often by a third or more. On the motorway the gap shrinks.

The Toyota has been making hybrids for longer than almost anyone, and its system is known for being efficient and extremely durable. The RAV4 hybrid is lively for its class, with a standard all-wheel-drive setup on many versions. Toyota also sells a plug-in version in some countries, which can drive a good distance on electric power alone before the petrol engine starts. If your daily trips are short and you can charge at home, it can cut your fuel bill a lot.

The Honda hybrid works in a slightly different way. At low and medium speed the petrol engine mostly acts as a generator, and the electric motor drives the wheels. The result is a smooth, quiet, almost electric feel in town, and a very relaxed pull when you accelerate. Fuel use is close to the Toyota's. Many drivers say the Honda feels more refined and the Toyota feels more energetic.

The non-hybrid petrol versions are different in character. The CR-V uses a small turbocharged engine, which is quick and easy to drive, but it adds complexity and is a little more sensitive to the quality of the fuel and the oil. The RAV4's plain petrol engine is a simpler design. It is less quick, but many people like the thought of fewer parts to go wrong.

Whichever you pick, real fuel use depends on how you drive. Speed, short trips in cold weather, heavy loads and a roof box all push consumption up. Treat the official figure as a guide for comparing cars, and not a promise of what you will get.

Space and comfort

This is the Honda's strongest point. The CR-V's rear seat has more legroom than the RAV4's and often more than most rivals. Adults can sit behind a tall driver without trouble, and the doors open wide, which makes it easy to fit a child seat. The floor is flat, and the high roof gives plenty of headroom.

The RAV4 is no cramped car, but its rear seat is a bit tighter, and the sloping window line makes the back feel slightly less airy. For most families this makes little difference. If you often carry three adults in the back or have tall teenagers, take a seat behind the driver and see for yourself.

Boot space is almost a draw. Both cars have a large, square boot that swallows a pram, a few suitcases or a week of shopping, and both fold the rear seats nearly flat for bigger loads. Look at the load height and the opening. A low floor and a wide opening make it easier to lift heavy items in. Some hybrid and plug-in versions lose a little space to the battery, so check the exact version.

For comfort, the Honda has softer seats for long trips, and the Toyota has seats that are firm and supportive. Neither is a mistake. Sit in both for more than a few minutes, and adjust the driving position. Check the visibility too, because thick pillars and a high rear window can make parking harder in one car than the other.

How they drive

Neither car is built to thrill, and both are easy and predictable to drive. The CR-V has a smoother, quieter character. Steering is light, the ride is soft over bumps, and the cabin is well insulated from wind and tyre noise. It is a very relaxing car for long motorway days.

The RAV4 feels a bit more eager and a bit firmer. Its ride is more controlled, with less roll in corners than you might expect from a tall car, though it passes bumps on to the cabin more than the Honda does. The engine can sound a bit coarse when you press hard, especially on the petrol versions. The hybrid is much quieter in everyday driving.

The hybrids of both cars use a brake system that blends regenerative and mechanical braking. Some drivers find the pedal feel a little unusual at first. It takes a few days to get used to, and it settles into habit.

On a test drive, try to include a rough road, a motorway stretch and a tight car park. Listen for wind noise and tyre noise, check how the car feels when you change lanes, and see whether you like the seating height. A short loop around the dealer's block tells you very little.

Screens and controls

The Honda's dashboard is clean and uncluttered. It has a real volume knob, real climate buttons and a tidy screen on top. Many drivers appreciate being able to change the temperature or the fan speed without looking away from the road. It feels like a design made by people who drive.

The Toyota has moved toward larger screens and more software in its newer versions. The screens are bright and the menus are improving, though some controls are inside the screen. As with all new cars, the quality of the software and the phone link varies by version. Check that the model you want supports the phone connection you use, for example wireless or wired phone mirroring, and test it with your own phone in the showroom.

Both cars include a full set of driver aids on most versions, such as adaptive cruise control that keeps a distance from the car in front, lane-keeping help, automatic emergency braking and blind spot warning. These systems differ in how gentle or sharp they are. Some drivers find lane-keeping too fussy, and all of them can be adjusted or switched off in the settings. Learn the controls so that the car helps you, and does not annoy you.

Safety

Both vehicles generally do very well in crash tests by independent organisations, and both come with a broad set of safety equipment as standard in many markets. Look up the current rating for the exact model year and the exact market, since test standards change and the results apply to the car as tested.

A crash rating is one piece of the picture. Useful things to check yourself are headlight quality, since poor lights are a real hazard on dark country roads, the number of airbags, rear seat belt reminders, child seat anchor points and how easy the anchors are to reach. Try fitting a child seat in the showroom if you have one.

If you buy a used car, check that safety recalls have been completed. A dealer or the maker can tell you from the vehicle identification number.

Reliability and known issues

Reliability is why people buy these two cars. In most large owner surveys, both brands finish near the top, with Toyota usually slightly ahead. A typical RAV4 or CR-V in decent condition will run for well over 200,000 kilometres with normal servicing, and many go much further.

No car is perfect, and a few points are worth knowing, especially for used buyers. Some older CR-Vs with the small turbocharged petrol engine had reports of fuel mixing with the engine oil in cold weather, which can thin the oil if the car is used for short trips in the cold. Honda has made changes since, and the problem was mostly seen in certain years. If you consider an older petrol CR-V, ask for the service history, check the oil level and the oil condition, and ask whether any updates were done.

On the Toyota side, some earlier RAV4 generations had issues with specific parts, and the newest models have the usual teething problems of any new design. As always, a model that has been on sale for a few years has had its early faults sorted out.

Hybrid batteries are another common worry. Both brands have a good record, and most hybrid batteries last far beyond what owners expect, with many cars running over 250,000 kilometres on the original battery. They also carry a long warranty on the hybrid parts in most markets. Still, ask for a battery health check when buying used, and ask what a replacement would cost.

The best predictor of reliability is how the car was treated. Regular oil changes, fresh brake fluid and coolant, and a documented service history matter more than the badge.

Rough roads, snow and towing

If you live where roads are poor, winters are snowy or you often drive on gravel, the RAV4 has a slight edge. Its ground clearance is a little higher on the rugged trim levels, and it has a reputation for coping better with rough tracks. Its all-wheel-drive systems on some versions can send power to the wheel that needs it, which helps in mud and snow. The CR-V's all-wheel drive is also capable in snow and on wet roads, but it sits a bit lower and is tuned more for comfort.

An all-wheel-drive car with summer tyres can be worse in snow than a front-wheel-drive car on proper winter tyres. Tyres matter more than the drivetrain. If you have real winters, budget for a set of winter tyres whichever car you choose.

For towing, the RAV4 typically has a higher rating, which makes it the better choice for a small trailer, a boat or a caravan. The CR-V can tow only light loads. Check the exact rating for the version you want and keep in mind that towing raises fuel use a lot, especially on a hybrid.

Neither one is a true off-road vehicle. They are comfortable family cars that can handle poor roads and light trails, and not rock crawlers.

The cost of owning one

Purchase prices of the two are close in most markets, with the hybrid and plug-in versions costing more than the plain petrol ones. A hybrid usually pays back the extra price over a few years if you drive a good amount in town. If you only do a few thousand kilometres a year, the saving takes much longer to add up.

Servicing is cheap for both. Parts are plentiful, mechanics know the cars well and they do not need expensive routine work. Both come with a standard warranty of several years, and many markets offer a longer warranty on the powertrain and the hybrid system. Read the terms for your country, because they differ.

Insurance, tyres and fuel make up most of the running cost. Larger wheels cost more to replace, so if you plan to drive on poor roads, a trim with smaller wheels and thicker tyres is cheaper and more comfortable. Fuel varies with the model, so use the official consumption figure and your own driving to estimate.

Resale value is a strong point for both, and it is one of the reasons people choose them. Used RAV4s in particular hold their value very well, and used CR-Vs are close behind. If you plan to sell in a few years, this will return a good part of your money.

If you buy used

Both cars are among the best used buys in their class, which also means demand is high and prices can be firm. A careful check protects you.

  • Ask for the full service history, with dates and distances. A car with regular oil changes is worth more than a car with fewer kilometres and no records.
  • Check the oil on the dipstick. It should be clean and at the right level, and it should not smell of petrol.
  • Look for rust on the underside and around the wheel arches, particularly if the car has lived somewhere with road salt.
  • Check the paint for signs of repair, such as uneven gaps between panels or colour differences. Ask if the car has been in an accident.
  • On a hybrid, ask for a battery health report, and check that the hybrid system shows no warning lights.
  • Test the air conditioning, the electric windows, the cameras and every button inside.
  • Check the vehicle identification number against the paperwork and look for open recalls.
  • Drive it cold if you can. Some problems show up in the first few minutes of a cold start.

Buying where support varies

In many countries, official sales and service are only part of the picture. Many cars arrive as used imports from other countries, and that adds a few questions of its own.

  • Parts and service. Toyota has a very wide service network in many regions and parts are widely available. Honda support varies more by country. Ask where the nearest trained mechanic is and how long common parts take to arrive.
  • Market version. A car built for another market may have different equipment, a different engine tune or different lighting. Some features, such as certain apps, may not work in your country.
  • Fuel quality. Check the fuel the engine needs and what is easy to find near you. A turbo petrol engine can be fussier about fuel quality than a simple engine, and poor fuel can cause trouble.
  • Gas conversion. In some countries many drivers fit their cars to run on natural gas or propane to save money. Turbo engines and hybrids are harder to convert, and a bad conversion can damage the engine or void the warranty. Talk to a qualified specialist before you plan on it.
  • Climate. Very hot summers wear out batteries and air-conditioning faster, and very cold winters make hybrids use more fuel. Ask owners in your area how their cars cope.
  • Paperwork. Make sure the import documents, customs payments and registration are all in order. A car with unclear papers can become a long and costly headache.

Who should pick which

The Toyota RAV4 makes more sense if

  • you want the strongest reliability record and resale value;
  • you want a plug-in hybrid option;
  • you drive on rough roads or in snow, or you tow a trailer;
  • you like a tougher, more rugged look;
  • service and parts for Toyota are easy to find where you live.

The Honda CR-V makes more sense if

  • you carry adults or children in the back often;
  • you value a quiet, smooth ride on long trips;
  • you like a cabin with real buttons and knobs;
  • you want a refined hybrid for daily driving;
  • you like a more subtle, grown-up design.

If you cannot decide, take both for a test drive on the same day, on the same road, and pay attention to how you feel after thirty minutes in each. The one you want to drive again is usually the right choice. Either way, you are choosing from two of the safest bets in the car market.

Questions people ask

Which is more reliable, the RAV4 or the CR-V?

Both have excellent records. Toyota is usually rated slightly higher in large owner surveys, but the difference is small and depends on the model year.

Which has more room?

The CR-V has more rear legroom. Boot space is about the same.

Is the hybrid worth the extra cost?

Usually, if you do plenty of city driving. The fuel savings are largest in stop-and-go traffic.

Which one is better in snow?

Both do well with all-wheel drive and winter tyres. The RAV4 has slightly more ground clearance, which helps on deep snow or rough tracks.

Which one tows more?

The RAV4, on most versions. Check the rating for the exact car.

Which holds its value better?

Both do very well. The RAV4 is usually a little stronger on the used market.

How long do the hybrid batteries last?

Often well beyond 200,000 kilometres. Both makers offer a long warranty on the hybrid parts, and a battery health check is a good idea for used cars.

Should I buy new or used?

A well-kept used car saves a lot of money, since both models last well. Check the service history, and be careful with imported cars that have unclear papers.
